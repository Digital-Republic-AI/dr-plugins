# Brainstorming and Disruptive Ideas - Disruptive Techniques: Speculative Futures

> Research Date: 2026-04-29

> Depth: detailed

## Overview

Thinking about futures is a strategic competence that goes far beyond prediction. While prediction tries to say "what will happen", speculative futures methodologies explore "what could happen" and "what we want to happen" — and use that difference to generate radically new ideas in the present. The central logic is that, by building vivid and plausible futures, we create contexts in which disruptive solutions stop seeming absurd and start seeming necessary.

This document covers the main speculative futures techniques applicable individually: Shell's Scenario Planning, Voros's Futures Cone, Backcasting, Design Fiction, Brian David Johnson's Sci-Fi Prototyping, weak signals scanning with STEEP/PESTLE, the futurist Pre-mortem, the "Newspaper of the Future" exercise and the extreme "What If" technique. For each technique, there is a step-by-step guide and applied examples.

The common thread is practical: how to use thinking about futures to generate disruptive ideas that can be executed today.

## 1. Scenario Planning (Shell Method / Pierre Wack)

### What it is

Scenario Planning was developed at Royal Dutch Shell by Pierre Wack starting in 1971. The central premise is that the future is not a single point to be predicted, but a distribution of possibilities to be explored. Instead of betting on a single future, the organization prepares robust strategies for multiple plausible futures.

The method became famous for having allowed Shell to anticipate the 1973 oil crisis and, later, the drop in prices in the 1980s — while its competitors were caught off guard. ([The man who saw the future](https://www.strategy-business.com/article/8220))

### Key concept: Axes of Uncertainty and the 2x2 Matrix

The heart of the method is identifying the **critical uncertainties**: high-impact, highly unpredictable factors that will shape the future environment. These are opposed to **predetermined elements** — predictable factors such as slow demographic trends or physical constraints.

The process of selecting the uncertainties involves:

1. Listing all the factors relevant to the context (economic, technological, social, political, environmental)
2. Classifying each factor on two axes: **potential impact** (high/low) and **degree of uncertainty** (high/low)
3. Selecting the two factors with the highest impact AND the highest uncertainty
4. Positioning them as the axes of a 2x2 matrix

This generates four quadrants, each representing a distinct and internally consistent future. ([Scenario Planning | Methodology | Envisioning](https://www.envisioning.com/methodology/scenario-planning))

### Step by Step

**Step 1 — Define the focal question**
Formulate the strategic question that guides the exercise. Example: "What will the urban mobility market in Brazil look like in 2040?"

**Step 2 — Gather factors and trends**
Do a broad scan of drivers using the STEEP framework (Social, Technological, Economic, Environmental, Political). List everything that may influence the focal question.

**Step 3 — Separate predetermined elements from critical uncertainties**
Classify each factor. Population aging is predetermined. The pace of electric vehicle adoption is a critical uncertainty.

**Step 4 — Choose the two axes**
Select the two uncertainties with the highest impact and the highest unpredictability. Example: X axis = "Government regulation" (permissive vs. restrictive); Y axis = "Technology adoption" (fast vs. slow).

**Step 5 — Name and narrate the four scenarios**
Each quadrant gets a memorable name and a coherent narrative. The name should evoke the essence of the world, not be technical. Classic Shell examples: "Market World" and "Global Lockdown".

**Step 6 — Develop the narrative details**
For each scenario, write a story in first or third person in the present tense. Describe: what happened in the preceding years to get here, how people live, what changed in companies, what tensions exist.

**Step 7 — Test strategies against the scenarios**
Evaluate each initiative or product under development: does it work well in all four scenarios? In only one? This reveals vulnerabilities and opportunities. ([Prepare for Uncertainty with Scenario Planning Framework](https://umbrex.com/resources/frameworks/marketing-frameworks/scenario-planning-framework/))

### Example Applied Individually

**Context**: You are developing an online education product and want to generate disruptive ideas.

**Chosen axes**:
- X axis: Trust in AI as an educator (high vs. low)
- Y axis: Fragmentation of the labor market (high vs. low)

**Four scenarios**:

| | Trusted AI | Distrusted AI |
|---|---|---|
| Fragmented market | "Digital Guilds": workers learn micro-credentials every 6 months with personalized AI tutors | "Master and Apprentice": return of intensive human mentorship; platforms are aggregators of human experts |
| Stable market | "Infinite School": AI replaces the traditional university; diplomas lose value | "Gold Credential": human certifications are worth more; platforms focus on assessment, not teaching |

By exploring the "Digital Guilds" scenario, you can generate the disruptive idea of a platform that sells 3-month learning tracks that expire — and that is a feature, not a bug, because the job market changes too fast for permanent curricula.

## 2. Futures Cone (Joseph Voros)

### What it is

The Futures Cone is a visual framework created by the Australian researcher Joseph Voros, based on earlier work by Henchey (1978) and Taylor (1990). It organizes the different types of future into nested categories, represented as a cone that widens as it advances in time — the more distant the future, the wider the range of possibilities. ([The Futures Cone, use and history](https://thevoroscope.com/2017/02/24/the-futures-cone-use-and-history/))

### The Categories of the Cone

The cone has seven categories, from the center (most probable) to the edge (least probable):

| Category | Description | Guiding question |
|---|---|---|
| Projected | Direct extrapolation of the present, "business as usual" | What happens if nothing changes? |
| Probable | Futures that will probably occur based on current trends | What is most likely to happen? |
| Plausible | Futures that could occur based on what we know about how the world works | What could happen? |
| Possible | Futures that may occur with knowledge we do not yet have | What could happen with new science? |
| Preferable | Normative futures — what should happen | What do we want to happen? |
| Undesirable | Negative preferable futures — what we do not want | What do we need to avoid? |
| Absurd/Preposterous | Futures that today seem impossible or ridiculous | What seems completely impossible? |

The "Absurd" category is strategic: the researcher James Dator stated that "any useful idea about the future should appear to be ridiculous". This means that today's preposterous futures often become tomorrow's probable futures.

### Step by Step

**Step 1 — Define the time horizon**
Choose a period: 5, 10, 20 or 50 years. The longer it is, the more the cone opens and the more room there is for preposterous futures.

**Step 2 — Start from the Projected**
Describe the future if nothing changes. This calibrates the group's starting point.

**Step 3 — Progressively advance through the cone**
Move from the most central category to the edges. With each layer, the question becomes more open. This progressively "opens up" the group's thinking.

**Step 4 — Deliberately explore the Absurd**
Set aside time for preposterous futures. Use brainstorming without judgment. Ask: "What would be true if this absurd future occurred? What changes would be necessary?" Often, by rationalizing an absurd future, insights emerge that apply to the present.

**Step 5 — Identify the Preferable**
After exploring the possibilities, ask: which of these futures do you want to build? This connects speculation to action. ([Futures Cone: what it is and its uses](https://tech4future.info/en/futures-cone/))

### Example Applied Individually

**Context**: You work in healthcare and want disruptive ideas for disease prevention.

Applying the cone to the theme "preventive health in 2040":

- **Projected**: Improved monitoring apps, health plans with incentives for gym membership
- **Probable**: Wearable sensors detect diseases 5 years before symptoms
- **Plausible**: A personalized gut microbiome sold as a subscription service
- **Possible**: Nanobots in the blood correct cellular anomalies in real time
- **Absurd**: The concept of "disease" ceases to exist because the human body is continuously updated like software

By exploring the absurd, you arrive at an idea that is plausible today: what would a minimum viable "body operating system" be? Which parameters would you routinely monitor and update, as you do with your smartphone?

## 3. Backcasting (Starting from the Desired Future)

### What it is

Backcasting is a planning methodology that begins by defining a desired future and works back to the present to identify the steps necessary to get there. Unlike forecasting, which extrapolates current trends to project the future, backcasting starts from where you want to be and asks: "What needs to be true for this to be possible?" ([Backcasting - Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Backcasting))

The method is especially powerful for generating disruptive ideas because it forces the thinker to escape the constraints of the present. By imagining a radically different future and then building the path in reverse, solutions emerge that incremental logic would never produce.

### Difference between Forecasting and Backcasting

| Dimension | Forecasting | Backcasting |
|---|---|---|
| Starting point | Present | Desired future |
| Direction of reasoning | Present → Future | Future → Present |
| Typical result | Incremental improvements | Systemic transformations |
| Main risk | Prisoner of the status quo | The future may be unrealistic |
| Best use | Operational planning | Disruptive innovation |

### Step by Step

**Step 1 — Define the desired future precisely**
Write a specific future statement, with a metric and a deadline. Vague: "We want to be market leaders." Specific: "By 2035, 80% of Brazilian adults have access to medical diagnosis in under 24 hours, at a cost of less than R$ 50."

**Step 2 — Describe that future in detail**
Answer: How do people live in this world? What changed in behavior, infrastructure, regulation, technology and culture to make this possible?

**Step 3 — Identify the prerequisites**
Ask: "What needs to be true for this future to exist?" List all the elements: technologies that need to exist, regulations that need to change, behaviors that need to be transformed, infrastructure that needs to be built. ([How to Use Backcasting](https://medium.com/@LearnChangeDo/how-to-use-backcasting-to-create-a-plan-for-achieving-a-desirable-future-0fa21e8af3e9))

**Step 4 — Work in time layers**
Organize the prerequisites by time horizon. What needs to exist in 2030 for 2035 to be possible? What needs to exist in 2027 for 2030 to be possible? Continue until you reach what needs to happen this year, this month, this week.

**Step 5 — Identify the bottlenecks and the levers**
Which prerequisites are the hardest to achieve? Which unlock several others? These are the high-leverage points — where an innovation has a disproportionate impact.

**Step 6 — Generate ideas for the bottlenecks**
For each bottleneck, brainstorm solutions. How can we create or accelerate this prerequisite? These solutions are the disruptive ideas generated by backcasting.

### Example Applied Individually

**Desired future**: "By 2038, programming education is universally integrated into Brazilian primary education, with 95% coverage."

**Working backwards**:

- 2038: Every school has a validated methodology, trained teachers, infrastructure and an integrated curriculum
- 2033: National program piloted in 50% of schools; curriculum approved by MEC
- 2030: Methodology tested and approved; 50,000 teachers trained; platform works offline
- 2027: Pilot in 500 schools; training of trainers at scale
- 2026 (today): **Bottleneck identified** — there is no programming teaching method that works with teachers who have no technical training and no stable internet

**Disruptive idea generated**: Create a completely offline programming teaching system, based on physical cards and group dynamics, that any literate teacher can apply without technical training — and that scales to teachers who train other teachers.

## 4. Design Fiction and Speculative Design (Dunne & Raby)

### What it is

Design Fiction is the practice of creating artifacts, objects, narratives and prototypes of worlds that do not yet exist, with the aim of provoking reflection and debate about possible futures. The term was coined by Bruce Sterling in 2005, but it was systematized by Julian Bleecker in 2009 and widely popularized by Anthony Dunne and Fiona Raby, of the RCA (Royal College of Art). ([Design fiction - Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Design_fiction))

The central concept is the **diegetic prototype**: an object that belongs to a fictional world and that, by existing materially in the real world, tells the story of that world without needing to explain it. Like the Tricorder from Star Trek — it did not require the script to explain the physics behind it. The audience understood its function from the context.

### Design Fiction vs. Speculative Design vs. Critical Design

| Approach | Focus | Intent | Typical context |
|---|---|---|---|
| Design Fiction | Narrative and artifacts of future worlds | Generate debate and explore possibilities | Corporate, research, public policy |
| Speculative Design (Dunne & Raby) | Questioning present assumptions via alternative futures | Cultural and philosophical critique | Galleries, academia |
| Critical Design | Challenging dominant values via design | Political provocation | Galleries, activism |

Dunne & Raby, in their book *Speculative Everything* (2013), propose that design can be a tool for imagining worlds that do not exist, dismantling existing mindsets by creating "objects from worlds that do not yet exist". ([Speculative Everything - MIT Press](https://mitpress.mit.edu/9780262019842/speculative-everything/))

### Design Fiction Formats

Design Fiction can take many physical and digital forms:

- Physical prototypes of fictional objects
- User manuals for products that do not exist
- Packaging and labels of future products
- Documentary videos of alternative worlds
- Comics or short stories
- Fictional crowdfunding campaigns
- Newspapers and magazines from alternative futures
- App interfaces that do not yet exist

### Step by Step

**Step 1 — Formulate the founding "What if..."**
Start with a clear and provocative speculative question. Examples: "What if the government monetized citizens' personal data instead of collecting taxes?" / "What if empathy could be measured and certified?"

**Step 2 — Build the world (worldbuilding)**
Answer the worldbuilding questions:
- Which rules changed in this world?
- Which social values dominate?
- Which tensions exist?
- How is the day-to-day life of an ordinary person different?

**Step 3 — Identify the diegetic artifact**
Which physical or digital object would be common in this world but strange in ours? This object should tell the story of the world just by existing.

**Step 4 — Create the prototype**
Fabricate the artifact realistically enough to suspend disbelief. A printed package, a PDF manual, an interface mockup, a short 2-minute video.

**Step 5 — Display it and provoke debate**
Present the artifact without an initial explanation. Observe the reactions. The questions that arise ("But how would this work?", "Would this be ethical?") reveal the tensions that the Design Fiction is mapping.

**Step 6 — Extract insights for the present**
Which ideas that emerged from the debate are applicable today? Which real problems does the fictional artifact solve? ([Full article: Beyond diegetic prototyping](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/14626268.2025.2480577))

### Example Applied Individually

**Founding "What if"**: "What if health plans priced insurance based on real-time behavioral data collected by the smartphone?"

**Diegetic artifact created**: A "Plan Adjustment Letter" — a printed notification in the style of a bank letter, with the following fictional content:

> *"Dear customer, following the analysis of your Healthy Behavior Index (HBI) for March 2031, your plan has been adjusted from Category B to Category C. Main factors: irregular sleep index (23 days), alcohol consumption above the parametric limit (4 occasions), sedentary behavior detected (fewer than 3,000 steps/day on 12 days). Your new monthly fee is R$ 1,840. To contest, access the portal and request an audit of your data."*

When this letter is presented in a brainstorming session, ideas immediately emerge: a "privacy insurance" that protects behavioral data; a platform that aggregates data from multiple users to negotiate collectively with insurers; a service that allows data to be selectively "sold".

## 5. Sci-Fi Prototyping (Brian David Johnson / Intel)

### What it is

Sci-Fi Prototyping is a methodology created by Brian David Johnson when he was Chief Futurist at Intel. The central idea is to use science fiction stories — written by anyone, not just professional writers — as prototypes of technological and social futures. According to Johnson: "it's science fiction based on science fact. It's used to prototype the future, and science fiction is about people." ([Sci-fi prototyping - Fast Company](https://www.fastcompany.com/90783469/sci-fi-prototyping-how-to-predict-the-future-and-prepare-for-the-unexpected))

The difference from Design Fiction is the format: while Design Fiction emphasizes physical artifacts, Sci-Fi Prototyping uses short textual narratives (short stories, comics, scripts) as its main vehicle. The difference from conventional science fiction is the intent: the prototypes are written to explore the implications of real technologies under development, not for entertainment.

Companies such as Nike, Ford, Boeing, Visa and PepsiCo use specialized firms such as SciFutures to apply the methodology. ([Science Fiction Prototyping - Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Science_fiction_prototyping))

### The Five Steps of the Methodology

**Step 1 — Establish the baseline (scientific fact)**
Research the real technology or trend that will be explored. Do not invent the science — start from something that exists or is under development. Example: language models that simulate the voices of deceased people.

**Step 2 — Project 10 years ahead**
Imagine how this technology will have evolved in a decade. What will become possible? What will become common? What will have stayed stuck in limitations?

**Step 3 — Imagine the social transformations**
How will this technology change behaviors, relationships, laws, the economy and culture? Who benefits? Who is harmed? What new problems arise?

**Step 4 — Humanize: tell one person's story**
Write a short narrative (1 to 5 pages) centered on a specific character living in this world. Do not describe the technology abstractly — show how it affects one person's day: a mother, a worker, a teenager. Specificity is what makes the prototype useful.

**Step 5 — Analyze and extract implications**
After writing the story, answer: Which problems arose that we had not anticipated? Which business or product opportunities appear? Which ethical or regulatory decisions are urgent? ([Science Fiction Prototyping - Medium / Sage Futuremakers](https://medium.com/sagefuturemakers/science-fiction-prototyping-32bc2763767a))

### Structure of the Sci-Fi Prototype Narrative

A well-built prototype has:

1. **Introduction**: the world in a few sentences — what has changed since today
2. **Character and situation**: who they are and what problem they face in this world
3. **Turning point**: the moment when the technology intervenes (for better or worse)
4. **Resolution or tension**: the outcome — which does not need to be positive
5. **Reflection (meta-commentary)**: the author's own closing paragraph making explicit what the prototype reveals

### Example Applied Individually

**Base technology**: Voice interfaces that simulate deceased people from recordings and messages

**Prototype narrative (summary)**:

> *2034. Mariana, 34, a lawyer in São Paulo. Her father died three years ago. Since then, she has been using the service "Presence" — an AI trained on 40 years of her father's messages, emails and video recordings. Today she has a difficult hearing and, as she always does, she calls him on the way to the courthouse. The conversation is comforting, familiar. Her father says exactly what she needs to hear. When Mariana arrives at the courthouse, she realizes that she has spent the last six months making every important professional decision by consulting an AI. And that she can no longer tell what her father would say from what she wants him to say.*

**Implications extracted**:
- Product: "Digital memory" service with ethical limits configurable by the user
- Regulatory risk: post-mortem image rights, prior consent
- Adjacent opportunity: AI-mediated grief therapy with clinical supervision

## 6. Weak Signals and Scanning with STEEP/PESTLE

### What it is

Weak signals are emerging indicators of change that have not yet become recognized trends — they exist only on the periphery, discussed by niche experts, researchers or pioneering communities. The systematic identification of these signals is the first step of any speculative futures exercise. ([Horizon scanning and futures thinking - UK Government Analysis Function](https://analysisfunction.civilservice.gov.uk/blog/horizon-scanning-and-futures-thinking-tools-for-government-analysts/))

The STEEP framework (Social, Technological, Economic, Environmental, Political) or PESTLE (Political, Economic, Social, Technological, Legal, Environmental) organizes signal scanning into domains, ensuring that no area is neglected.

### Difference between a Weak Signal and a Trend

| Level | Characteristic | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Weak signal | Known only to experts and pioneers; no consensus; scarce data | First reports of microplastics in human blood (2018) |
| Emerging trend | Growing coverage in specialized media; initial studies published | Microplastics regulations being debated (2022-2023) |
| Megatrend | Widely recognized; systemic impact confirmed | Sustainability as an investment criterion (2024+) |

### Step by Step of Signal Scanning

**Step 1 — Define the scope**
Determine the domain of interest (health, mobility, work, food) and the time horizon (5, 10, 20 years).

**Step 2 — Create a map of sources by STEEP category**

| STEEP category | Recommended sources |
|---|---|
| Social | Behavior reports, demographic data, values surveys (Gallup, Pew Research) |
| Technological | Preprints (arXiv, bioRxiv), VC reports, technical conferences (NeurIPS, CVPR) |
| Economic | Central bank reports, fintechs, commodity data |
| Environmental | IPCC reports, climate disaster data, biodiversity research |
| Political | Legislative agendas, international treaties, speeches by emerging leaders |

**Step 3 — Collect signals without filtering**
Set aside a period (1 hour, 1 week) for broad scanning. Note anything unusual, surprising or that challenges your current assumptions. Do not discard something because it seems improbable.

**Step 4 — Classify the signals**
For each signal collected, assess: What is the current strength of the signal (weak/medium/strong)? Which STEEP category does it fit into? What is the impact horizon?

**Step 5 — Identify convergences**
Signals from multiple STEEP categories pointing in the same direction indicate robust trends. A political + technological + social signal converging on the same phenomenon is more reliable than an isolated signal. ([Using STEEP to Frame Your Horizon Scanning](https://www.insightandforesight.com.au/blog-foresights/using-steep-to-frame-your-horizon-scanning))

**Step 6 — Formulate future hypotheses**
For each convergence, write a hypothesis: "If this signal strengthens, in X years, what will be true?" These hypotheses feed the scenarios, Backcasting and Design Fiction.

### Example Applied Individually

**Domain**: Food and food production

Weak signals identified in 2024-2025:
- (Technological) Lab-grown proteins approved for sale in Singapore and the US
- (Social) Growth of "freegan" and urban composting communities in Brazilian cities
- (Economic) Hedge funds betting against traditional animal protein companies
- (Environmental) First reports of fish population collapse in previously productive areas
- (Political) Traditional farmers' lobbies pushing for restrictive regulations on alternative proteins

**Convergence identified**: Growing tension between the industrial food system and regenerative/synthetic alternatives.

**Future hypothesis**: "By 2035, there will be a 'food apartheid' — traditional foods will be premium and luxury, while synthetic proteins will be the accessible foods."

**Disruptive idea generated**: A synthetic food brand that positions itself as "the haute cuisine of the future" — reversing the narrative that synthetic is inferior.

## 7. Futurist Pre-mortem

### What it is

The Pre-mortem is a technique created by the psychologist Gary Klein, first described in 1991 and popularized by a Harvard Business Review article in 2007. In its original form, the pre-mortem serves to identify project risks: you imagine that the project has failed and ask why.

The **futurist** version of the pre-mortem applies the same logic to idea generation: instead of analyzing a specific project, you imagine that a vision of the future did not come true — and the analysis of the causes of failure reveals the obstacles that need to be removed and, consequently, the innovations needed to overcome them. ([Premortem | Gary Klein](https://www.gary-klein.com/premortem))

The cognitive mechanism is **"prospective hindsight"**: studies show that imagining that an event has already happened can double the number of causes identified compared with simply asking "what could go wrong?"

### Step by Step

**Step 1 — Define the vision of the future**
Write a specific desired future: "By 2035, our company is the leading preventive health platform in Brazil, with 10 million active users."

**Step 2 — Project to the moment of failure**
Declare: "It is 2035. This vision did not come true. The company did not even come close. What happened?"

**Step 3 — Individual (silent) generation of causes**
Each participant writes down individually, without speaking, all the possible causes of the failure. Silence is critical — it prevents one dominant voice from contaminating the group. Time: 10 minutes.

**Step 4 — Share in rounds**
Each person shares one cause at a time, in rounds. The facilitator lists everything without debate. Continue until the lists are exhausted.

**Step 5 — Identify the highest-impact causes**
Vote or prioritize: which causes, if they occurred, would be the most devastating to the vision?

**Step 6 — Invert into opportunities**
For each critical cause of failure, ask: "What innovation or initiative would eliminate this risk? What product or service would make this failure future impossible?" These answers are disruptive ideas. ([Pre-mortem - Ness Labs](https://nesslabs.com/pre-mortem-anticipate-failure-with-prospective-hindsight))

### Example Applied Individually

**Vision**: "By 2032, Rio de Janeiro's public transport is the most efficient in Latin America."

**Failure causes identified in the pre-mortem**:
1. Corruption in bidding contracts delays all works by 5 years
2. Climate change causes frequent floods that destroy newly built infrastructure
3. Users do not adopt the new system because they do not trust its punctuality
4. The system was planned for the passenger volume of 2024, but the city grew unexpectedly

**Disruptive ideas generated from the causes**:
- Cause 1: Public contract audit platform on blockchain, with real-time citizen access
- Cause 2: Transport infrastructure designed to be modular and relocatable in the face of floods
- Cause 3: Punctuality guarantee with automatic credit to the user's card if the bus is late
- Cause 4: Transport system that expands organically like cellular networks (without rigid central planning)

## 8. Newspaper of the Future ("Newspaper from the Future")

### What it is

The "Newspaper of the Future" is a futures thinking exercise in which participants write headlines and articles for a fictional newspaper set in a specific future. The technique combines forward-looking thinking with journalistic narrative — forcing participants to describe the future not as an abstraction, but as concrete facts that have already occurred. ([Headlines from the Future - SessionLab](https://www.sessionlab.com/methods/headlines-from-the-future))

The exercise works because the journalistic format has clear conventions: headlines are factual, not speculative; articles answer the questions who, what, when, where and why. This forces the thinker to move out of generality ("AI will transform everything") and land on specificity ("company X launches service Y that does Z, impacting W people").

### Step by Step

**Step 1 — Define the scenario and the horizon**
Choose: which future will be the context of the newspaper? It can be a specific scenario from a scenario planning exercise, or a desired future from backcasting. Example: "The newspaper is from September 15, 2038. We live in a world where energy is abundant and free."

**Step 2 — Create the newspaper masthead**
Give the newspaper a name (it can be an existing or fictional newspaper), the date and the edition. This anchors the exercise in reality.

**Step 3 — Write individual headlines (10 minutes)**
Each participant writes 3 to 5 headlines from the newspaper of that future. Rules: use the past tense ("Brazilian company has become..."), be specific (names, numbers, places), and mix positive and negative headlines.

**Step 4 — Develop an article (optional, 15 minutes)**
Expand a headline into a short article of 200 words. This deepens the exploration and reveals implications that the headline does not capture.

**Step 5 — Share and identify themes**
Each person reads their headlines. The group identifies recurring themes and tensions — what appears in several headlines? What contradicts other headlines? Which futures emerge that no one had anticipated?

**Step 6 — Extract implications for the present**
Which headlines describe problems that would need solutions today? Which describe solutions that could be developed now? ([Predict Future Headlines - Re-imaginary](https://www.reimaginary.com/methods/predict-future-headlines))

### Example Applied Individually

**Scenario**: 2036, a world with extremely cheap and accessible solar energy.

**Headlines generated**:

1. "Bitcoin miners abandon data centers in Texas and migrate to the deserts of Brazil's Northeast"
2. "Northeast water crisis reversed: solar desalination system processes 2 billion liters per day"
3. "Energy distribution sector collapses: utilities lose 80% of their value in 3 years"
4. "Rural communities in Amazonas become exporters of energy credits"
5. "WHO declares mental health crisis: 'technological idleness' affects 40% of displaced industrial workers"

**Disruptive ideas extracted from headline 4**: A cooperative platform that allows rural communities with excess solar energy to sell credits directly to urban companies — a peer-to-peer energy market that disintermediates the utilities.

## 9. Extreme "What If"

### What it is

The Extreme "What If" technique consists of formulating radical hypothetical questions that challenge the fundamental assumptions of a sector, product or business model. The "extreme" version goes beyond conventional questions ("what if we improved the speed?") and proposes structural transformations ("what if money ceased to exist?"). ([How to Facilitate the 'What If?' Innovation Technique - Big Bang Partnership](https://bigbangpartnership.co.uk/what-if-technique/))

The technique is especially effective for generating disruptive ideas because it forces a temporary abandonment of the constraints of the present. Ideas generated by extreme "What Ifs" are rarely directly applicable, but they often reveal strategic directions that can be implemented incrementally.

### Types of Extreme "What If"

| Type | Structure | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Inversion | "What if the opposite were true?" | "What if customers paid to NOT use our product?" |
| Elimination | "What if this core element did not exist?" | "What if there were no money in healthcare?" |
| Extreme scale | "What if this were 1000x bigger/smaller?" | "What if our company served 8 billion people at the same time?" |
| Transfer | "What if the rules of X applied to Y?" | "What if healthcare worked like a streaming subscription?" |
| Time collapse | "What if the future had already arrived?" | "What if everyone already had access to expert-level medical AI?" |
| Unlikely agent | "What if [unexpected actor] controlled this market?" | "What if IBGE managed all private health data?" |

### Step by Step

**Step 1 — Define the core system or problem**
Be specific: not "the future of healthcare", but "how people choose doctors in Brazil today".

**Step 2 — List the fundamental assumptions**
What are the assumptions that are never questioned in this system? List 5 to 10. Example for choosing doctors: "Doctors are chosen by the health plan", "Reputation is built through word of mouth", "The appointment takes place in an office".

**Step 3 — Invert or radicalize each assumption**
For each assumption, formulate a "What If" that radically questions it. Example: "What if doctors chose their patients, not the other way around?" / "What if reputation were built on clinical outcome data, not on referrals?"

**Step 4 — Generate ideas without judgment**
For each "What If", generate 3 to 5 product, service or business model ideas that would make sense in that hypothetical world. Judgment is forbidden at this stage.

**Step 5 — Identify the most fertile ideas**
Which ideas generated by the extreme "What If" are partially applicable today? Which point to a genuine strategic direction? Which reveal a real problem that no one is solving?

**Step 6 — Build bridges to the present**
For the most fertile ideas, ask: what minimum version of this idea could we test in 3 months? What would need to be true for this idea to scale? ([50 what-if questions to reimagine the future - Board of Innovation](https://www.boardofinnovation.com/staff_picks/50-what-if-questions-to-reimagine-the-future/))

### Example Applied Individually

**System**: Higher education in Brazil

**Fundamental assumption**: "Diplomas are the currency of professional credibility"

**Extreme "What If"**: "What if university diplomas were illegal — if they became a prohibited form of labor discrimination?"

**Ideas generated**:
1. A dynamic portfolio platform that replaces the resume with documented real projects
2. A "skills audit" service that validates competencies through timed practical challenges
3. A marketplace where companies publish real problems and hire whoever solves them — with no screening by diploma
4. A hiring-risk insurer: the company pays a premium and is guaranteed that the candidate without a diploma will perform equivalently to one with a diploma, or receives a refund

**Minimum version testable today**: A LinkedIn group of companies that publicly commit to not requiring a diploma for specific positions — and measure the results.

## How to Connect the Techniques: An Integrated Flow

The nine techniques are not mutually exclusive — they feed each other in sequence. A typical flow for individual use:

```
STEEP/PESTLE
(identify weak signals and trends)
        |
        v
Futures Cone
(organize the possible and preferable futures)
        |
        v
Scenario Planning (Shell 2x2)
(build 4 coherent narratives of the future)
        |
        v
Backcasting (from the preferable future)
(identify the prerequisites and bottlenecks)
        |
        v
Extreme "What If" / Futurist Pre-mortem
(generate disruptive ideas for the bottlenecks)
        |
        v
Newspaper of the Future
(make the ideas concrete and narrative)
        |
        v
Design Fiction / Sci-Fi Prototyping
(prototype the futures to provoke debate and refine)
```

For individual use in short sessions, any technique can be used in isolation. It is recommended to start with the **Newspaper of the Future** (30 minutes, low cognitive cost, high concreteness) or with the **Extreme "What Ifs"** (20 minutes, applicable to any immediate context) as an entry point.

## Key Takeaways

1. **Speculative futures do not predict — they liberate.** The main function of these techniques is not to guess the future, but to escape the mental prisons of the present. By building plausible futures, ideas that seem impossible today become logically necessary.

2. **The Futures Cone and Shell's Scenario method are complementary.** The Cone organizes the space of possible futures; the Scenarios narrate the most relevant ones. Use the Cone first to open up thinking, then the Scenarios to deepen it.

3. **Backcasting is the bridge between speculative futures and action plans.** The other techniques explore futures; Backcasting converts exploration into strategy, identifying the bottlenecks that need innovation.

4. **Design Fiction and Sci-Fi Prototyping make futures tangible.** Physical artifacts and human narratives are more effective than slide presentations for provoking genuine reflection and identifying unanticipated implications.

5. **Weak signals (STEEP) are the fuel for all the other techniques.** Without systematic scanning of emerging trends, scenarios and "What Ifs" remain anchored in current assumptions — exactly what the techniques try to overcome.

6. **The futurist Pre-mortem and the Newspaper of the Future are the most accessible techniques for beginners.** They require less prior knowledge, have quick results and work well individually without specialized facilitation.

7. **Extreme "What Ifs" generate the most radical ideas, but need a bridge to the present.** The technique is effective for breaking paradigms, but the step of building testable versions is essential — without it, the ideas stay in the realm of fantasy.

8. **Speculative futures reveal problems that do not yet exist — and that is their value.** The disruptive idea that emerges from a 2040 scenario often points to a problem that exists in embryonic form today, but that no one has seen as an opportunity.
