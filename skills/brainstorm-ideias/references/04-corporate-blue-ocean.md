## Overview

Blue Ocean Strategy is a methodology developed by professors W. Chan Kim and Renée Mauborgne of INSEAD, originally published in 2005 after the analysis of more than 150 strategic moves across 30 industries over 100 years. The central premise is simple and powerful: instead of competing in a saturated market (red ocean), it is possible to create an entirely new market space (blue ocean) where competition becomes irrelevant.

What sets Blue Ocean Strategy apart from other innovation approaches is the concept of Value Innovation: the simultaneous pursuit of differentiation and cost reduction. In conventional logic, a company must choose between offering more value (at a higher cost) or cutting costs (delivering less). Blue Ocean breaks this trade-off by questioning which competing factors are truly valued by the buyer and which are merely historical legacies of the industry.

This document covers the four practical pillars of the methodology: the Strategy Canvas (visual diagnosis of the battlefield), the ERRC Grid (tool for redesigning the value proposition), the Six Paths Framework (six paths to identify new oceans) and the Three Tiers of Noncustomers (map of latent demand). At the end, a worked example demonstrates how to apply the frameworks individually to rethink a product, service or career.

## Red Ocean vs. Blue Ocean

The ocean metaphor structures the entire methodology and is the starting point for any practical application.

| Dimension | Red Ocean | Blue Ocean |
|---|---|---|
| Market space | Existing, known | New, unexplored |
| Competition | Intense, for market share | Irrelevant |
| Demand | Fought over among competitors | Created |
| Strategic logic | Beat the competition | Make the competition irrelevant |
| Value-cost relationship | Trade-off: more value = more cost | Breaks the trade-off |
| Value proposition | Aligned with the industry standard | Radically different |

In red oceans, industry boundaries are accepted as given and companies try to outperform rivals within those boundaries. As the space gets more crowded, prospects for profit and growth shrink. Blue oceans, by contrast, are defined by uncontested market space, by the creation of demand and by the opportunity for highly profitable growth. Most blue oceans are created from within red oceans by redrawing industry boundaries. ([Blue Ocean Strategy - Official Site](https://www.blueoceanstrategy.com/what-is-blue-ocean-strategy/))

### Value Innovation: the core of the strategy

Value Innovation is what separates a blue ocean strategy from a mere technological or niche innovation. Value innovation occurs when the company aligns innovation with utility, price and cost position. This means:

- **Eliminate and Reduce** factors that the industry has always offered but that do not create real value for the buyer (reducing costs)
- **Raise and Create** factors that the industry has never offered and that the buyer truly values (raising perceived value)

The result is a value curve that diverges radically from the competitors' curve, making direct comparison impossible. When value innovation is well executed, both costs and buyer value improve simultaneously. ([HBR - Blue Ocean Strategy](https://hbr.org/2004/10/blue-ocean-strategy))

## Strategy Canvas: the Visual Diagnosis

The Strategy Canvas is the central diagnostic and action tool of Blue Ocean Strategy. It is a one-page chart that captures the current state of an industry's competitive field and serves as the starting point for imagining the future.

### How to read the Strategy Canvas

The canvas operates on two axes:

- **Horizontal axis**: lists the industry's competing factors — the attributes on which the industry's companies compete and invest (e.g., price, service quality, product variety, location, brand experience)
- **Vertical axis**: shows the level of offering buyers receive on each factor — a scale from low to high

Each company (or strategic group) is represented by a **value curve**: a line connecting the company's performance points on each factor. When all the companies in an industry have similar value curves, this is a clear sign of a red ocean — everyone competing on the same dimensions, at the same levels. ([Strategy Canvas - Blue Ocean Strategy Tools](https://www.blueoceanstrategy.com/tools/strategy-canvas/))

### As-Is Canvas and To-Be Canvas

The Strategy Canvas serves two distinct purposes:

**As-Is Canvas:** captures the current competitive landscape. It reveals where competitors invest, which factors the industry competes on and what value existing offerings deliver. It exposes the dynamics of the red ocean: similar value curves, strategies that imitate each other.

**To-Be Canvas:** visualizes how a blue ocean strategic move breaks with the existing market reality. The new value curve must have three qualities:

1. **Focus**: the strategy emphasizes a few factors, instead of trying to be good at everything
2. **Divergence**: the curve diverges from the competitors' curves, instead of imitating them
3. **Compelling tagline**: the strategy can be summed up in a clear sentence that makes sense to the target market

### How to build a Strategy Canvas (step by step)

**Step 1 - List the industry's competing factors**
Research what the industry's companies actually do to attract customers. Include tangible factors (price, speed, variety) and intangible ones (prestige, ease of use, support).

**Step 2 - Evaluate the competitors**
For each factor, rate the main competitors' level of offering on a scale of 1 to 5 (or low-medium-high). Plot their value curves.

**Step 3 - Evaluate your current position**
Plot your own current value curve on the same chart. Notice where you follow the industry standard and where you diverge.

**Step 4 - Identify patterns and opportunities**
Where are all the curves similar? Those points indicate where the industry has competed blindly without questioning the value delivered. Where are there gaps that no player addresses?

**Step 5 - Apply the ERRC Grid (see next section)**
Use the ERRC to redesign your value curve, creating the To-Be Canvas.

**Step 6 - Validate the To-Be Canvas**
Does the new curve have focus, divergence and a compelling tagline? If so, you have a blue ocean strategy. ([StrategyU - Blue Ocean Strategy](https://strategyu.co/blue-ocean-strategy/))

### Example: Cirque du Soleil

The most cited case of Blue Ocean Strategy is Cirque du Soleil, created by Guy Laliberté in Canada. In the early 1990s, the circus industry was in decline. Companies competed on the same factors: star performers, animal shows, three simultaneous rings, popular prices for families.

**As-Is Canvas of the circus industry (factors and profiles)**

| Competing factor | Traditional circus (large) | Regional circus (small) |
|---|---|---|
| Star performers | High | Low |
| Animal shows | High | Medium |
| Number of rings | High (3) | Low (1) |
| Ticket price | Medium | Low |
| Themed experience | Low | Low |
| Artistic production | Low | Low |
| Original music | Low | Low |
| Sophisticated setting | Low | Low |

Cirque du Soleil did not try to be a better circus. Instead, it looked to an alternative industry (adult theater and Broadway) and completely redesigned the concept. Its To-Be Canvas diverged radically:

- **Eliminated**: star performers, animal shows, multiple rings
- **Reduced**: fun and humor (present, but not the central focus), danger and adrenaline
- **Raised**: the acrobatic and physical skill of the performers, comfort of the setting
- **Created**: artistic theme with narrative, sophisticated setting, original music, theatrical production

The result was a proposition aimed at adults willing to pay theater prices — not at families looking for cheap entertainment. Cirque's value curve has focus (few raised factors), divergence (completely different from competitors) and a compelling tagline: "A world-class theatrical production with the skill and wonder of the circus." ([Shortform - Cirque du Soleil Blue Ocean Strategy](https://www.shortform.com/blog/cirque-du-soleil-blue-ocean-strategy/))

## ERRC Grid: the Redesign Tool

The ERRC Grid (Eliminate-Reduce-Raise-Create) is the operational tool that turns the Strategy Canvas diagnosis into concrete actions. It is a simple four-quadrant matrix that forces the company (or the individual) to act in all four directions simultaneously. ([ERRC Grid - Blue Ocean Strategy Tools](https://www.blueoceanstrategy.com/tools/errc-grid/))

### The four quadrants

```
+---------------------------+---------------------------+
|         ELIMINATE         |           RAISE           |
|                           |                           |
| Which factors that the    | Which factors should be   |
| industry has always taken | raised well above the     |
| for granted should be     | industry standard?        |
| eliminated?               |                           |
+---------------------------+---------------------------+
|          REDUCE           |          CREATE           |
|                           |                           |
| Which factors should be   | Which factors that the    |
| reduced well below the    | industry has never offered|
| industry standard?        | should be created?        |
+---------------------------+---------------------------+
```

**Eliminate**: questions factors the industry offers out of inertia, not because buyers truly value them. Eliminating these factors reduces costs.

**Reduce**: identifies areas where the industry "over-delivers" — investing more than buyers need. Reducing frees up resources without harming perceived value.

**Raise**: uncovers compromises that buyers are forced to accept in current offerings. Raising these factors solves real pain points.

**Create**: explores factors the industry has never offered, often imported from alternative industries. Creating opens up new sources of value.

The strength of the ERRC lies in operating on all four quadrants simultaneously: Eliminate and Reduce lower costs; Raise and Create increase value for the buyer. This is what makes value innovation possible.

### Why use all four quadrants

Many companies fail by using only half of the grid. Focusing only on Raise and Create without Eliminate and Reduce results in higher costs without sustainable differentiation. Focusing only on Eliminate and Reduce without Raise and Create results in a cheaper offering, but without a distinct value proposition. The power is in the whole. ([ERRC Grid Template and Examples - Blue Ocean Strategy](https://www.blueoceanstrategy.com/blog/errc-grid-template-examples/))

### Worked example: [yellow tail] Wine

The case of [yellow tail] wine from Casella Wines (Australia) is one of the most didactic examples of applying the ERRC Grid.

**Context**: The North American wine market was highly competitive. Producers competed on flavor complexity, aging, winery prestige, technical vocabulary and variety of options. The result was a product that was intimidating to the casual consumer.

**Strategic question**: Instead of competing with French and Californian wines on the same ground, how to create a new market space?

**ERRC Grid application:**

| Eliminate | Reduce |
|---|---|
| Technical terminology and enology | Variety of options (only 2 labels: Chardonnay and Shiraz) |
| Winery prestige and legacy | Flavor complexity |
| Aging as an attribute | Mid-range price band |
| Marketing aimed at connoisseurs | |

| Raise | Create |
|---|---|
| Ease of drinking (smooth, accessible flavor) | Fun, social experience |
| Packaging accessibility (colorful, informal label) | Appeal to non-wine drinkers (beer, cocktails) |
| Retail availability | Simplified selection |

**Result**: Casella Wines expected to sell 25,000 cases in its first year of operation in the US (2001). It sold 225,000 — nine times more. [yellow tail] made wine accessible to people who had never bought wine, converting beer and cocktail drinkers into wine consumers. ([ERRC Grid - Medium Case Studies](https://medium.com/@pranavbhatblog/errc-grid-for-blue-ocean-strategy-explained-with-case-studies-7dabf70b1a44))

### Worked example: CitizenM Hotels

**Context**: Hotels competed on formal reception, concierge, valets, bellhops, in-house restaurants and room size — all high-cost factors.

| Eliminate | Reduce |
|---|---|
| Traditional reception (front desk) | Room size |
| Concierge, bellhops | In-hotel food services |
| Large rooms | |

| Raise | Create |
|---|---|
| Quality of the bed and bed linen | Self-service check-in (kiosks) |
| Shower pressure and quality | Multi-function "ambassadors" for guest support |
| Soundproofing | Lobby as a social and work space |
| Access to digital entertainment in the room | Sophisticated design aesthetic |

**Result**: CitizenM operates with costs 40% lower than comparable four-star hotels and 50% fewer employees than the industry average, while earning the highest guest ratings among chains in its category and achieving a 90% room occupancy rate — well above the industry average. ([ERRC Grid Examples - Blue Ocean Strategy Blog](https://www.blueoceanstrategy.com/blog/errc-grid-template-examples/))

## Six Paths Framework: six paths to new oceans

The Six Paths Framework is the methodology for identifying where to look for a blue ocean. While the ERRC Grid defines what to change, the Six Paths defines where to look. The six paths help reconstruct market boundaries in a systematic way. ([Six Paths Framework - Blue Ocean Strategy Tools](https://www.blueoceanstrategy.com/tools/six-paths-framework/))

### Path 1 - Look across alternative industries

**Conventional strategic logic**: compete with rivals within the same industry.
**Blue ocean logic**: examine why buyers choose alternatives from other industries.

When someone needs to relax on an afternoon, they might choose to go to the movies, a restaurant, a park or stay home streaming. These are alternative industries that serve the same buyer goal. By understanding which factors lead buyers to move between alternatives — and eliminating everything else — it is possible to create new market space.

**Example**: Cirque du Soleil looked at adult theater (an alternative industry to the circus) and asked: what do people value in theater that the circus does not offer? The answer led to the creation of thematic narrative, original music and sophisticated production.

**Practical questions**:
- What are the alternative industries to your offering?
- Why do buyers choose one over another?
- Which factors determine that choice?
- What can you incorporate from those alternatives?

### Path 2 - Look across strategic groups within the industry

**Conventional strategic logic**: improve competitive position within one's own strategic group.
**Blue ocean logic**: understand what makes buyers migrate between strategic groups.

Within almost every industry there are strategic groups — companies that compete with similar strategies (e.g., luxury vs. mainstream brands in fashion; premium gyms vs. low-cost gyms). Path 2 asks: what leads customers from one group to consider the other?

**Example**: The Curves gym chain (1990s) observed that women who wanted to exercise did not feel comfortable in traditional gyms (mixed, intimidating, complex). By combining the low cost of budget gyms with a women-only environment and 30-minute circuits, it created a blue ocean — reaching more than 10,000 locations in 60 countries.

**Practical questions**:
- What are the strategic groups in your industry?
- What makes a luxury customer consider a mainstream option (or vice versa)?
- Which factors are valued in each group that the other ignores?

### Path 3 - Look across the chain of buyers

**Conventional strategic logic**: serve the conventional target buyer better.
**Blue ocean logic**: redefine who the buyer is.

In most industries, there is a chain of buyers: the purchaser (who pays), the user (who uses) and the influencer (who recommends or specifies). Companies usually focus on only one of these groups. Shifting the focus can reveal entirely new opportunities.

**Example**: The pharmaceutical company Novo Nordisk realized that, in the insulin market, the focus was always on doctors (influencers). By shifting the focus to patients (users), it developed NovoPen — an insulin injector pen that replaced syringes and vials. The product completely changed the user experience and generated a multibillion-dollar market.

**Practical questions**:
- Who are the purchasers, users and influencers in your chain?
- Whom does your industry typically focus on?
- What happens if you shift the focus?
- What problems does the ignored group have that are not being solved?

### Path 4 - Look across complementary product and service offerings

**Conventional strategic logic**: maximize the value of the product/service within the limits of the category.
**Blue ocean logic**: examine the context in which the product is used — what happens before, during and after.

Most products and services are not used in isolation. There are complementary activities that take place around the usage experience. By solving the problems that occur in this broader context, it is possible to create new value.

**Example**: NABI created tablets for children by considering not just the device, but the complete ecosystem: parental controls, educational content, age-appropriate games, durability (rubberized case), easy charging. Each element solved a problem in a child's context of use, not just in the "tablet" category.

**Practical questions**:
- What does the buyer do before, during and after using your offering?
- What pains or inconveniences exist in this broader context?
- What complementary product or service could eliminate those pains?

### Path 5 - Look across functional or emotional orientation

**Conventional strategic logic**: improve price-performance within the industry's existing functional or emotional orientation.
**Blue ocean logic**: question that orientation and eventually invert it.

Some industries compete mainly on functional (rational) performance: price, efficiency, durability. Others compete on emotional appeal: prestige, identity, aesthetics. Questioning this orientation can reveal unexpected opportunities.

**Example**: Swatch transformed watches from a functional accessory (telling the time) into a fashion accessory and expression of personality — moving a functional category into the emotional field. Conversely, The Body Shop transformed cosmetics (typically emotional/aspirational) into a functional and ethical offering, rejecting luxurious packaging and glamour appeals.

**Practical questions**:
- Does your industry compete on function or emotion?
- What happens if you invert that orientation?
- Which customer segment would benefit from that inversion?

### Path 6 - Look across time

**Conventional strategic logic**: adapt to external trends as they appear.
**Blue ocean logic**: identify trends and actively take part in shaping their direction.

Every industry is subject to external trends — technological, regulatory, behavioral, demographic. Most companies respond to these trends reactively. The sixth path proposes identifying trends with a high probability of impact and projecting what the market will look like when that trend matures — acting ahead of time.

**Example**: Apple identified that the digitization of music (trend) would create a distribution problem. In 2003, it launched iTunes while the industry was still debating piracy, creating a digital distribution market that sold billions of songs at US$0.99 each — a blue ocean in music distribution.

**Practical questions**:
- Which trends have a high probability of impacting your field in the next 5-10 years?
- Which trend is already under way but has not yet reached critical mass?
- If this trend consolidates, what will your industry look like?
- What can you offer today for that future world?

([Six Paths Framework - Collaborat.com](https://www.collaborat.com/the-six-paths-framework-in-formulating-blue-ocean-strategy/))

## Three Tiers of Noncustomers: mapping unmet demand

One of the most powerful questions in Blue Ocean Strategy is: who is not buying our product — and why? Most companies concentrate resources on serving existing customers better. Blue ocean strategy reverses this focus and examines the three tiers of noncustomers as the primary source of insights for creating new demand. ([Three Tiers of Noncustomers - Blue Ocean Strategy](https://www.blueoceanstrategy.com/tools/three-tiers-of-noncustomers/))

### First tier: "soon-to-be" noncustomers (Soon-to-be)

These are people at the edge of the current market. They use the industry's offering minimally, out of necessity or lack of a better alternative — not because they really want to. They are mentally disengaged and ready to move to a different solution if one appears.

**Identification signal**: customers who use the product but are dissatisfied, or who progressively reduce their use.

**Example in the wine context**: consumers who drank wine only at formal dinners because it was socially expected, but would prefer beer or a cocktail if they could. [yellow tail] captured exactly this group.

### Second tier: noncustomers who refuse (Refusing)

These are people who consciously evaluated the industry's offering and rejected it. They know the product/service exists, but chose not to participate because the current offering does not meet their needs or values.

**Identification signal**: people who know the category but never buy, with clear reasons for refusing.

**Example**: Adults who never went to gyms because they consider them intimidating, expensive or incompatible with their routine — but who would like to exercise. Peloton and other at-home fitness platforms converted part of this group.

### Third tier: unexplored noncustomers (Unexplored)

These are the most distant noncustomers. They were never considered potential customers by the industry, and the industry itself never thought of them as a target. They solve their needs in completely different ways — or don't solve them at all.

**Identification signal**: population groups that have the need your category could serve, but that were never mapped as a market.

**Example**: When Nintendo launched the Wii in 2006, the video game industry was aiming at young males (hardcore gamers). The Wii opened up to seniors, families and non-gamers — a third tier of noncustomers that no manufacturer had considered. The motion controller made gameplay intuitive for those who had never touched a video game.

### How to use the Three Tiers in practice

1. List the three groups of noncustomers in your category
2. For each group, research the reasons they do not buy (interviews, surveys, observation)
3. Identify commonalities among the groups — what all of them find problematic in the current offering
4. These commonalities are the most powerful value levers for creating a blue ocean

([INSEAD Knowledge - Noncustomer Analysis](https://knowledge.insead.edu/strategy/how-create-your-blue-ocean-through-noncustomer-analysis))

## Buyer Utility Map: mapping where to create value

The Buyer Utility Map complements the Three Tiers by providing a structure for identifying where, in the buyer's experience, there are opportunities to create new utility. It is a 6x6 matrix that combines the stages of the buyer experience with the utility levers. ([Buyer Utility Map - Blue Ocean Strategy](https://www.blueoceanstrategy.com/tools/buyer-utility-map/))

### The six stages of the buyer experience

1. **Purchase**: how the buyer discovers, evaluates and acquires the product
2. **Delivery**: how the buyer receives or accesses the product
3. **Use**: the experience of using the product/service
4. **Supplements**: what the buyer needs beyond the main product
5. **Maintenance**: how the product is maintained, updated or renewed
6. **Disposal**: how the product is discarded at the end of its useful life

### The six utility levers

1. **Customer productivity**: does the product make the buyer more efficient or productive?
2. **Simplicity**: is the product simple to use, understand and acquire?
3. **Convenience**: is the product available when and where the buyer needs it?
4. **Risk**: does the product reduce financial, physical or reputational risks?
5. **Fun and image**: is the product enjoyable, aesthetically appealing or does it strengthen the buyer's identity?
6. **Environmental friendliness**: does the product have a low environmental impact?

### How to use the Buyer Utility Map

Mark on the map where the industry's current offering delivers utility and where there are gaps (blank cells = opportunities). A blank cell in a position relevant to noncustomers is a blue ocean opportunity.

**Simplified example — travel insurance industry:**

| Stage / Lever | Purchase | Delivery | Use | Supplements | Maintenance | Disposal |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Productivity | Low (long forms) | - | Medium | Low | - | - |
| Simplicity | Low (complex terms) | Low | Low | Low | - | - |
| Convenience | Low (limited channels) | Medium | Low (hard to file a claim) | - | - | - |
| Risk | Medium | - | Medium | - | - | - |
| Fun and image | - | - | - | - | - | - |
| Friendliness | - | - | - | - | - | - |

The blank and low-value cells indicate where to invest. Simplifying the purchase and the filing of claims would be a starting point for value innovation in this industry.

([Buyer Utility Map - Shortform](https://www.shortform.com/blog/buyer-utility-map/))

## Individual Application: Blue Ocean for career and personal projects

Blue Ocean Strategy is not exclusive to large corporations. The same frameworks apply to individual careers, career projects, freelance services and professional positioning. The logic is identical: instead of competing on the same dimension as every other professional in your field, create a space where direct comparison becomes irrelevant. ([Blue Ocean Strategy - Career Development](https://www.blueoceanstrategy.com/blog/career-development-plan/))

### The "me-too résumé" trap

Most professionals develop a profile indistinguishable from their peers in the same field — the value curves are almost identical. When that happens, the market decides based on price (salary) or connections, not differentiated value. This is the red ocean of careers.

### Strategy Canvas for a career (worked example)

**Hypothetical scenario**: UX designer with 3 years of experience wanting to reposition their career.

**Common competing factors in the UX Design field:**
- Project portfolio
- Mastery of tools (Figma, Adobe XD)
- User research skills
- Front-end knowledge (HTML/CSS)
- UX certifications
- Communication soft skills
- Business and metrics knowledge
- Specialization in a specific industry

**As-Is (current profile vs. market):**

| Factor | Market average | Current profile |
|---|---|---|
| Portfolio | High | Medium |
| Tools | High | High |
| User research | Medium | Medium |
| Front-end | Low | Low |
| Certifications | Medium | Medium |
| Communication | Medium | High |
| Business knowledge | Low | Low |
| Industry specialization | Low | Low |

The current curve is similar to the market's — direct competition.

**Applying the ERRC to create the To-Be:**

| Eliminate | Reduce |
|---|---|
| Focus on generic certifications | Diversity of tools (focus on 2-3) |
| Portfolio of varied projects without a common thread | |

| Raise | Create |
|---|---|
| Business and metrics knowledge (OKRs, conversion) | Deep specialization in UX for B2B SaaS |
| User research (qualitative and quantitative methods) | Design documentation and writing (design writing) |
| Communication with stakeholders | Product strategy + design consulting |

**To-Be Canvas**: the new profile would be that of a UX designer specialized in B2B SaaS, with a strong ability to connect design decisions to business results and the skill to communicate in executive language. This profile has focus (B2B SaaS), divergence (not a UX generalist) and a compelling tagline: "UX Designer who speaks the language of business."

### Three Tiers of noncustomers applied to the career

Who doesn't hire you and why?

- **Soon-to-be**: companies that hire UX designers but are frustrated because the professionals do not connect design to business results
- **Refusing**: SaaS companies that outsource design to agencies because they cannot find professionals with enough product context
- **Unexplored**: early-stage startups that never considered hiring a dedicated designer because they thought they could not afford it

The To-Be Canvas created above addresses the first two groups and potentially the third with a flexible consulting model.

## Complete Example: Applying the Frameworks to a Service

### Scenario: Personal productivity consulting

**Context**: market saturated with productivity coaches and consultants, competing for the same audience with the same promises (GTD, Pomodoro, task organization).

#### Step 1 - As-Is Strategy Canvas

The industry's competing factors:
- Hourly price (high vs. low)
- Variety of methodologies
- Digital presence (courses, social media)
- Coaching certifications
- One-on-one sessions
- Teaching material (templates, spreadsheets)
- Follow-up between sessions
- Specialization by client profile

All competitors have similar curves: high on digital presence and methodological variety, low on specialization and follow-up.

#### Step 2 - Six Paths to identify where to innovate

- **Path 1 (alternatives)**: What do people looking for productivity do instead of hiring a consultant? They watch YouTube, buy books, take online courses. The most-used alternative is free content.
- **Path 3 (chain of buyers)**: Who pays? Usually the person themselves. Who influences? The manager or the company's HR. Shifting the focus to HR as the customer could open a corporate market unexplored by individual consultants.
- **Path 5 (functional-emotional)**: Productivity is sold emotionally (achievement, success, balance). What if it were offered as a functional service with measurable metrics (time ROI)?

#### Step 3 - ERRC Grid

| Eliminate | Reduce |
|---|---|
| Generic coaching sessions | Variety of methodologies (focus on 1-2) |
| Standardized teaching material (generic templates) | Presence on multiple social networks |
| Certifications as a selling point | |

| Raise | Create |
|---|---|
| Specialization in a specific profile (technical leaders at startups) | Quantitative diagnosis of time use (calendar audit) |
| Continuous follow-up between sessions | Productivity metrics dashboard for the client |
| Explicit connection to business results | Results-based contract model (not hours-based) |

#### Step 4 - To-Be Canvas and tagline

**New positioning**: "Productivity consulting for technical leaders at startups — data-driven diagnosis, measurable results."

**Divergence**: specialization + quantitative metrics + results-based compensation model

**Focus**: three raised factors (specialization, follow-up, metrics) vs. seven factors in the conventional market

**Tagline**: "You know where your time goes. We help you get it back."

#### Step 5 - Three Tiers of noncustomers

- **Soon-to-be**: founders who have already tried productivity coaches but felt a lack of connection to concrete results
- **Refusing**: CTOs and technical leaders who consider coaching "vague" and prefer data-based solutions
- **Unexplored**: technology companies that never thought of leaders' productivity as a measurable investment — only as HR training

The new positioning especially converts the second group (who refuse the conventional offering) and potentially the third.

## Key Takeaways

1. **Red ocean vs. blue ocean is a conscious strategic choice**: you can decide to stop competing on the same factors as everyone else and create a space where direct comparison is impossible. The first step is always an honest diagnosis of your current red ocean.

2. **Value Innovation is not "more for less" — it is different**: value innovation is not about being cheaper or about being more premium. It is about eliminating what does not create value and creating what no one yet offers. Without the ERRC Grid working across all four quadrants, there is no real value innovation.

3. **The Strategy Canvas reveals the red ocean visually**: when you plot the competitors' value curves and they look almost identical, you are looking at a red ocean. That is the diagnosis. The To-Be Canvas is the prescription.

4. **The Six Paths are lenses, not recipes**: use them as opening questions, not as a checklist. Each path can reveal a different opportunity; the value is in asking the right questions and looking beyond the conventional boundaries of your industry.

5. **Noncustomers are the main source of insights for new oceans**: most companies (and professionals) are so focused on serving existing customers better that they ignore the enormous latent demand in the three tiers of noncustomers. The biggest blue ocean is usually where no one is looking.

6. **Individual application is completely valid**: Blue Ocean frameworks apply to careers, personal projects and freelance services as effectively as to large corporations. The logic is identical: create a profile or offering that diverges from the market standard, with focus and a compelling tagline.

7. **The sequence matters**: start with the diagnosis (As-Is Strategy Canvas), explore where to look (Six Paths), understand the unmet demand (Three Tiers), redesign the proposition (ERRC Grid) and validate the result (To-Be Strategy Canvas). Skipping steps produces incomplete strategies.
