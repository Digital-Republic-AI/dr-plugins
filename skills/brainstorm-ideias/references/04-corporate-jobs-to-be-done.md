# Brainstorm and Disruptive Ideas - Corporate Innovation: JTBD, ODI and Disruption Theory

> Depth: detailed
> Language: en (translated from pt-BR)

## Overview

This document covers three interlinked frameworks that form the foundation of customer-driven innovation: Jobs-to-be-Done (JTBD), Outcome-Driven Innovation (ODI) and Clayton Christensen's Disruption Theory. Although they originated in different ways, the three share a central principle: understanding what the customer really wants to accomplish — not what they ask for, not what the company wants to sell, but the progress they are trying to make in their life.

For beginners, these frameworks radically change the way of thinking about products and businesses. Instead of asking "what can I build?", you start asking "what job is my customer trying to do, and how can I do it better than any available alternative?" This inversion of perspective is what makes the three frameworks so powerful for generating product, service or business model ideas.

This document presents each framework from scratch, with practical examples, usable templates and guidance for personal application — even without a team or a corporate budget.

## Jobs-to-be-Done (JTBD)

### What a "Job" Is

Jobs-to-be-Done is a theory developed by Clayton Christensen, professor at Harvard Business School, and co-developed in practice by Bob Moesta. The central premise is that people do not buy products — they "hire" products to do a specific job in their lives. The "job" is the progress a person is trying to make in a given circumstance. ([Christensen Institute](https://www.christenseninstitute.org/theory/jobs-to-be-done/))

A job is not a feature, not a demographic segment and not a persona. It is a unit of analysis that describes what the customer is trying to accomplish — functionally, emotionally and socially. The theory holds that understanding the job precisely is the most reliable path to creating products that people actually adopt.

### The Three Types of Jobs

Every job has three dimensions that must be understood together for effective innovation. ([SIVOINSIGHTS - How to Identify Functional, Emotional & Social Jobs](https://mrx.sivoinsights.com/blog/how-to-identify-functional-emotional-and-social-jobs-using-the-jobs-to-be-done-framework))

#### Functional Jobs

They are the basic, measurable task the person wants to complete. It is the practical, objective, outcome-oriented aspect.

Examples:
- "Track my daily workouts" (fitness app)
- "Transport my belongings from one city to another" (moving company)
- "Have a hole in the wall to hang a picture" (drill)

The functional job answers the question: **what does the customer want to do, concretely?**

#### Emotional Jobs

They are how the customer wants to feel while performing the task. They reflect subjective, personal motivations that go beyond practical utility.

Examples:
- "Feel confident presenting in a new outfit"
- "Reduce the stress of booking a trip that will go smoothly"
- "Feel motivated to keep training" (running shoes)

The emotional job answers the question: **how does the customer want to feel during and after the experience?**

#### Social Jobs

They are how the customer wants to be perceived by others. They are tied to identity, status and belonging to a group.

Examples:
- "Choose a luxury vehicle to signal success"
- "Buy eco-friendly packaging to look environmentally responsible"
- "Signal dedication to a healthy lifestyle" (premium running shoes)

The social job answers the question: **how does the customer want to be seen by others when using this product?**

> Key insight: "Functional jobs tell you what to build. Emotional and social jobs often tell you why it matters to people." ([SIVOINSIGHTS](https://mrx.sivoinsights.com/blog/how-to-identify-functional-emotional-and-social-jobs-using-the-jobs-to-be-done-framework))

### The Milkshake Example

The milkshake case is the most famous and didactic example of JTBD theory. A fast-food chain wanted to increase milkshake sales. The traditional approach: segment the market by product and demographics, ask customers about the characteristics of the ideal milkshake, improve the product. Result: minimal impact. ([Clay Christensen's Milkshake Marketing - HBS Working Knowledge](https://www.library.hbs.edu/working-knowledge/clay-christensens-milkshake-marketing))

The research team tried a different approach: a researcher spent 18 hours inside a McDonald's watching who bought milkshakes, when, with what, and alone or accompanied. The findings were surprising:

- Almost half of daily sales occurred between 6:30 and 8:30 in the morning
- Buyers were always alone, inside the car
- It was the only item purchased — nothing else in the order

**The real job of the morning milkshake was not "to drink something tasty". It was:**
- To keep a hand occupied during a long, boring commute to work
- To have something that took time to consume (the thin straw made the process slow — this was an advantage)
- To stave off hunger until lunch without the risk of a bagel or doughnut crumbling onto clothes

The real competition was not other milkshakes: it was banana, cereal bar, bagel, coffee. The product that won this market was not the "best milkshake" by conventional criteria — it was the one that best did the job of "surviving the boring commute". ([Milkshakes in the Morning - The Rewired Group](https://therewiredgroup.com/case-studies/milkshakes/))

**What the chain did after understanding the job:**
- Made the milkshake even thicker and slower to consume
- Added fruit pieces to make it more interesting
- Result: sales increased 7 times

### The Template: Job Statement

The Job Statement is the standardized way of writing an identified job. The structure is:

```
Action verb + object + context/circumstance
```

**Job Statement examples:**

```
"Organize my personal finances at the end of the month without making mistakes"
"Learn English during work breaks without needing a teacher"
"Share trip photos with the family simply and quickly"
```

Avoid putting a solution in the job statement. "Use an app to..." already steers toward a specific solution and closes off possibilities.

### Job Stories: The Template for Product

Alan Klement adapted JTBD to the product development context with the **Job Story** format. The structure is:

```
When [situation/context], I want to [motivation/force], so that I can [expected outcome].
```

**Examples:**

```
When I am driving to work and I feel the morning is going to be long,
I want something that keeps me awake and entertained,
so that I can arrive at the office more prepared and less bored.

When I finish a difficult project at work,
I want public recognition from my team,
so that I can feel that my effort was noticed and worth it.

When I need to present results to the board,
I want the data to be organized visually and clearly,
so that I can look competent and prepared.
```

The Job Story is superior to the traditional User Story ("As a [persona], I want [feature], so that [benefit]") because it captures the **situational context** that triggers the behavior — the most predictive element of adoption. ([Digital Leadership - Jobs To Be Done](https://digitalleadership.com/blog/jobs-to-be-done/))

## Switch Interview: Bob Moesta's Four Forces

### What the Switch Interview Is

The Switch Interview is the research technique developed by Bob Moesta to uncover the real job behind a purchase decision. The focus is not on the product, not on features, not on satisfaction — it is on the **moment of the switch**: the instant the customer decided to "fire" the current solution and "hire" a new one. ([Bob Moesta on Intercom](https://www.intercom.com/blog/podcasts/bob-moesta-on-unpacking-customer-motivations-with-jobs-to-be-done/))

The fundamental premise: understanding why someone switched reveals more about the real job than any satisfaction survey or focus group.

### The Four Forces of Progress

Bob Moesta mapped four forces that act simultaneously at the moment of any switching decision. Two forces drive the change; two hold it back. ([Unpacking the Progress Making Forces Diagram - JTBD](https://jobstobedone.org/radio/unpacking-the-progress-making-forces-diagram/))

| Force | Direction | Description |
|-------|-----------|-------------|
| Push | Favors change | The problem or dissatisfaction with the current situation that makes the person want to leave |
| Pull | Favors change | What attracts the person to the new solution — the promise of progress |
| Anxiety | Against change | Doubts and fears about adopting something new — "what if it doesn't work?" |
| Habit | Against change | The force of inertia — "this is how I've always done it" |

For a change to happen, the Push + Pull forces must overcome Anxiety + Habit. If Habit and Anxiety are stronger, the customer stays where they are — even if the new product is objectively better. ([The Forces of Progress - Innodyn](https://innodyn.net/the-forces-of-progress/))

> "Nobody really wants to change. Habit is the strongest force of all, and people will keep doing what they do unless something gets in the way or something better comes along." ([Bob Moesta - Business of Software](https://businessofsoftware.org/talks/understanding-your-customer-jtbd/))

### Four Forces Example: Switching Personal Finance Software

Imagine someone deciding to switch from a spreadsheet to a personal finance app:

**Push:** "My Excel spreadsheet freezes when I have many tabs open. I keep losing data. At the end of the month it's stressful."

**Pull:** "The app promises to categorize expenses automatically. It seems much simpler than updating everything manually."

**Anxiety:** "I'll have to migrate two years of data. What if the app shuts down and I lose everything? At least I control my spreadsheet."

**Habit:** "I've always used a spreadsheet. My father taught me this way. It works. It's not ideal, but it's mine."

Understanding these four forces allows a product to reduce Anxiety (offering easy import and data export) and reinforce Pull (showing quick results), instead of just adding features.

### Switch Interview Structure (47 minutes)

The interview follows a chronological progression: it starts well before the purchase and runs through to current use. ([How to run a JTBD interview - june.so](https://www.june.so/blog/how-to-run-a-jtbd-interview-like-the-co-creator-of-the-framework))

| Step | Duration | Focus |
|------|----------|-------|
| 1. Setting the stage | 2 min | Create a casual, non-judgmental atmosphere |
| 2. Interviewee context | 2 min | Understand the person's role and routine |
| 3. First thought | 3 min | Identify the trigger moment — when did the idea of switching first come up? |
| 4. The struggle | 4 min | Explore the pain that drove the search — the Push |
| 5. Search for solutions | 5 min | How did the person research alternatives? What did they consider? |
| 6. Why this product? | 5 min | What tipped the decision — the Pull |
| 7. First experience | 5 min | Was the promise fulfilled? First impressions |
| 8. Evolution of use | 7 min | How has usage changed over time? |
| 9. Current frustrations | 9 min | What still bothers them — next potential Push |
| 10. Mapping the Forces | 5 min | Synthesis of the insights into Push, Pull, Anxiety, Habit |

### Example Switch Interview Questions

```
Push questions (identify the struggle):
- "Can you tell me about the first time you thought 'I need to change this'?"
- "What was happening in your life at that time?"
- "What was the last straw?"

Pull questions (identify the attraction):
- "What made you consider [new solution] specifically?"
- "What caught your attention about it?"
- "How did you imagine it would be different?"

Anxiety questions (identify the fears):
- "Was there a moment you almost gave up?"
- "What worried you about adopting it?"
- "What made you hesitate?"

Habit questions (identify the inertia):
- "How did you do it before? For how long?"
- "What were you going to lose by switching?"
- "Did anyone around you use something different? Why hadn't you switched earlier?"
```

## Outcome-Driven Innovation (ODI) by Tony Ulwick

### What ODI Is

Outcome-Driven Innovation is a strategic methodology developed by Anthony Ulwick in 1991 and refined over decades of projects at Fortune 1000 companies. The central premise: "If Jobs-to-be-Done is the theory, then ODI is the practice." ([Anthony Ulwick - Outcome-Driven Innovation](https://anthonyulwick.com/outcome-driven-innovation/))

The fundamental difference from conventional JTBD is that ODI goes beyond identifying the job — it **quantifies** which parts of the job are underserved and overserved. Instead of brainstorming or intuition, ODI uses data to point to where the real opportunity is.

The logic: when customers perform a job, they use internal metrics to judge whether they are being successful. Ulwick calls these metrics **desired outcomes**. Identifying, structuring and quantifying these outcomes is the core of ODI. ([Outcome-Driven Innovation - Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Outcome-Driven_Innovation))

### The Job Map: Eight Universal Steps

The Job Map is a central ODI tool that breaks any job down into eight universal steps. It does not show what the customer is doing (solution view) — it shows what the customer is trying to accomplish (needs view). ([Digital Leadership - ODI](https://digitalleadership.com/blog/outcome-driven-innovation/))

| Step | Name | What the customer does |
|------|------|------------------------|
| 1 | Define and Plan | Creates an initial strategy of approach |
| 2 | Locate Inputs | Identifies the information needed for the decision |
| 3 | Prepare | Organizes, filters and qualifies the information |
| 4 | Confirm and Validate | Makes decisions and validates them |
| 5 | Execute | Carries out the action based on the decisions |
| 6 | Monitor | Tracks the effects and results |
| 7 | Modify | Evaluates based on monitoring; adjusts if necessary |
| 8 | Conclude | Finishes the journey and extracts lessons learned |

The Job Map is iterative: customers can loop between steps, and different customers go through the same job in different ways. The usefulness of the map lies in revealing **where in the job the greatest frustrations are** — and therefore the greatest opportunities.

**Applied Job Map example: "Learn a new language"**

| Step | What the learner does |
|------|----------------------|
| Define and Plan | Decides which language, target proficiency level, available time |
| Locate Inputs | Researches courses, apps, teachers, methods |
| Prepare | Buys materials, installs apps, schedules classes |
| Confirm and Validate | Takes a diagnostic test, assesses whether the method seems suitable |
| Execute | Studies, practices, does exercises |
| Monitor | Tracks progress, takes tests, notices gaps |
| Modify | Changes method if not progressing |
| Conclude | Applies the language in a real situation (travel, conversation, work) |

### Desired Outcomes: The Correct Format

A Desired Outcome Statement is not a product requirement, not a feature and not a complaint. It is a metric the customer uses to evaluate success when performing the job. ([Digital Leadership - ODI](https://digitalleadership.com/blog/outcome-driven-innovation/))

**Structure of a Desired Outcome Statement:**

```
[Direction of improvement] + [Measure] + [Object] + [Context]
```

**Examples:**

```
"Minimize the time needed to find the right vocabulary when writing a formal email"
"Increase pronunciation accuracy when talking with native speakers"
"Reduce the anxiety of forgetting content already learned after periods without studying"
"Increase confidence when speaking in high-pressure situations such as business meetings"
```

Each statement is rated by customers on two dimensions (scale of 1 to 10):
- **Importance**: how important this outcome is to them
- **Current satisfaction**: how well existing solutions meet it today

### The Opportunity Score: Where the Real Opportunity Is

The Opportunity Score is the central ODI formula. It combines importance and satisfaction to reveal where the underserved opportunities are (and where the market is saturated). ([What Is Opportunity Scoring - airfocus](https://airfocus.com/glossary/what-is-opportunity-scoring/))

**Formula:**

```
Opportunity Score = Importance + max(Importance - Satisfaction, 0)
```

The logic: if something is very important and poorly satisfied, the opportunity is large. If something is important but already well served, there is no room for differentiation. If something is of low importance, it does not matter how dissatisfied the customer is — it is not a relevant opportunity.

**Score interpretation:**

| Score | Interpretation |
|-------|----------------|
| 15 or more | Highly attractive opportunity — underserved market |
| 10 to 14 | Attractive opportunity for some segments |
| Below 10 | Overserved market — innovating here is difficult and risky |

**Calculation example:**

```
Desired Outcome: "Minimize the time to correct grammatical errors in formal text"

Average importance: 8.5 / 10
Average satisfaction: 4.0 / 10

Opportunity Score = 8.5 + max(8.5 - 4.0, 0)
                  = 8.5 + 4.5
                  = 13.0 → Attractive opportunity
```

### How to Apply ODI Personally

Even without access to large research samples, it is possible to use the ODI logic to generate product or business ideas:

1. **Choose a job** you know well — as a user, as a professional, or through close observation
2. **Map the job** into the eight steps of the Job Map
3. **List 15 to 30 desired outcomes** for each relevant step
4. **Interview 5 to 10 people** who perform this job and ask them to rate the importance and satisfaction of each outcome
5. **Calculate the Opportunity Score** of each outcome
6. **Focus on the highest scores** — those are the real gaps in the market

ODI turns idea generation from a random creative process into a systematic, data-based process. ([Outcome-Driven Innovation - Venturae](https://www.venturae.tech/en/odi))

## Christensen's Disruption Theory

### What Disruptive Innovation Is

Disruption Theory was developed by Clayton Christensen starting in the mid-1990s and popularized in the book "The Innovator's Dilemma" (1997). The theory describes a recurring pattern: how entrant companies manage to displace established incumbents, despite starting with products that are inferior by the market's traditional evaluation criteria. ([Disruptive Innovation Theory - Christensen Institute](https://www.christenseninstitute.org/theory/disruptive-innovation/))

The precise definition: disruptive innovation describes a process by which a product or service takes root in simple applications at the bottom of the market — typically being less expensive and more accessible — and then relentlessly moves up-market, until it displaces established competitors. ([Wikipedia - Disruptive Innovation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Disruptive_innovation))

A frequently misunderstood point: not every radical innovation or new technology is disruptive in Christensen's sense. Disruption is a **process** with a specific pattern, not an intrinsic quality of a product.

### Sustaining Innovation vs. Disruptive Innovation

| Dimension | Sustaining Innovation | Disruptive Innovation |
|-----------|-----------------------|-----------------------|
| Goal | Improve existing products for current customers | Create new markets or enter from the bottom |
| Origin | Established companies | Usually entrants |
| Initial quality | Better on the metrics that are valued | Worse on traditional metrics |
| Initial price | Usually more expensive or similar | Usually cheaper |
| Initial target | Premium customers | Underserved customers or non-consumers |
| Trajectory | Incremental improvement on the same axis | Improves on different dimensions until it captures the mainstream |

Incumbents are excellent at sustaining innovation — it is what their business model incentivizes. The innovator's trap occurs because ignoring the bottom of the market (where the margin is low) seems rational in the short term, but it opens space for disruptors that grow and eventually move up-market. ([What Is Disruptive Innovation Theory - HBS Online](https://online.hbs.edu/blog/post/4-keys-to-understanding-clayton-christensens-theory-of-disruptive-innovation))

### The Two Types of Disruption

#### Low-End Disruption

It occurs when an entrant offers a simpler, cheaper product that serves customers who are **overserved** by the incumbent market. The incumbent does not fight for the bottom because it is not very profitable — and by retreating upward, it progressively cedes space.

**Classic example:** Netflix vs. Blockbuster
- Netflix started with DVDs by mail — convenient, but without the selection and immediacy of the store
- Blockbuster focused on its high-value customers (new-release fans, same-day rental)
- Netflix improved progressively, added streaming, and Blockbuster lost relevance
- ([ITONICS - Disruptive Innovation Explained](https://www.itonics-innovation.com/blog/disruptive-innovation-explained))

**Pattern:** The incumbent has overserved customers (who pay for more than they need). The disruptor serves the cheapest slice with a "good enough" product. The incumbent retreats. The disruptor improves and moves up.

#### New-Market Disruption

It occurs when an entrant creates an affordable solution for people who **did not consume** the product before — whether due to lack of money, knowledge or access.

**Classic example:** Personal computers vs. mainframes
- Mainframes were expensive, complex, and required specialized operators
- PCs were technically weak but allowed ordinary individuals to use computers for the first time
- The PC market grew until it was larger than the mainframe market
- ([Medium - From sustaining to new-market disruption](https://medium.com/design-bootcamp/from-sustaining-to-new-market-disruption-innovation-a-brief-overview-192ffcc75436))

**Pattern:** There is a job that is not being served because existing solutions are inaccessible. The disruptor creates a simple, accessible version. A new market of non-consumers starts to consume. Incumbents ignore it because the market "doesn't exist yet".

### Other Examples of Disruption

| Disruptor | Displaced incumbent | Type | Mechanism |
|-----------|---------------------|------|-----------|
| Wikipedia | Encyclopædia Britannica | New-market | Free, collaboratively created content for non-subscribers |
| Digital photography | Photographic film (Kodak) | Low-end | Inferior quality at first, but zero cost per photo |
| Smartphones | PDAs and digital cameras | New-market | Convergence of functions for non-users of each gadget |
| Airbnb | Luxury hotels | Low-end | Cheaper lodging for travelers who would not pay for a hotel |

### The Innovator's Dilemma and Why Incumbents Fail

Christensen identified that incumbents do not fail out of incompetence — they fail by rationally following the management practices that made them successful. This paradox is the "innovator's dilemma":

1. **Listening to the best customers** — who always want improvements to the premium product
2. **Focusing on the higher margins** — which are at the top of the market, not the bottom
3. **Allocating resources to the highest return** — which favors sustaining innovations

A disruptor that emerges from the bottom seems insignificant at first. By the time incumbents notice the threat, the disruptor has already dominated the entry segment and is moving up. ([Christensen Institute - Disruptive Innovation](https://www.christenseninstitute.org/theory/disruptive-innovation/))

### How to Use Disruption Theory to Generate Ideas

Disruption theory can be used as a search lens for business ideas:

**Lens 1 — Look for overserving:**
"Is there any product or service that people use but find excessive for their needs? Who pays for a premium package but uses only 20% of the features?"

**Lens 2 — Look for non-consumers:**
"Is there a job that many people wanted to do but don't because the available solution is expensive, complex or inaccessible? Who was left out of the market?"

**Lens 3 — Track the trajectory:**
"Can a simple, cheap solution progressively improve until it meets the needs of the mainstream market? Or is there an insurmountable technical ceiling?"

## JTBD Canvas: Synthesis Tool

The JTBD Canvas is a visual tool that consolidates research insights into a structured format for product decisions. It combines elements of JTBD with the concepts of ODI and the Forces of Progress. ([UXtweak - JTBD Templates](https://www.uxtweak.com/jobs-to-be-done/templates/))

### JTBD Canvas Structure

```
+---------------------------+---------------------------+
| MAIN JOB                  | CONTEXT                   |
| (what the customer does)  | (when / where / with whom)|
+---------------------------+---------------------------+
| FUNCTIONAL JOBS           | EMOTIONAL JOBS            |
| - ...                     | - ...                     |
| - ...                     | - ...                     |
+---------------------------+---------------------------+
| SOCIAL JOBS               | CURRENT ALTERNATIVES      |
| - ...                     | (what they "hire" today)  |
| - ...                     | - ...                     |
+---------------------------+---------------------------+
| PUSH (what pushes         | PULL (what attracts to    |
| away from the current)    | the new solution)         |
| - ...                     | - ...                     |
+---------------------------+---------------------------+
| ANXIETY (what holds back) | HABIT (what keeps them    |
| - ...                     | on the current)           |
| - ...                     | - ...                     |
+---------------------------+---------------------------+
| DESIRED OUTCOMES          | OPPORTUNITY SCORE         |
| (customer metrics)        | (importance - satisfaction)|
| - ...                     | - ...                     |
+---------------------------+---------------------------+
```

### Filled-In Example: Personal Expense Tracking App

**Main Job:** Know exactly where my money goes every month

**Context:** Professional aged 28-35, with variable or fixed income but a feeling that "the money disappears", usually alone at home at the end of the month

| Section | Content |
|---------|---------|
| Functional Jobs | Categorize expenses; identify unnecessary recurring expenses; forecast available balance |
| Emotional Jobs | Feel control and security; reduce financial anxiety; feel responsible |
| Social Jobs | Be seen as someone organized; avoid the embarrassment of an overdrawn account |
| Current Alternatives | Excel spreadsheet; banking app (incomplete); nothing (ignores the problem) |
| Push | "Every month the last week arrives and I don't know if I have the money for the bill" |
| Pull | "An app that categorizes automatically and shows a chart would be much simpler" |
| Anxiety | "Will I have to connect my bank account? What about security?" |
| Habit | "I already have my spreadsheet. It works, it's tedious, but it works" |
| Desired Outcomes | Minimize manual categorization time; increase accuracy in balance forecasting |
| Opportunity | Manual categorization: importance 9 / satisfaction 3 → score 15 (high opportunity) |

## How to Apply It Personally to Generate Ideas

### Four-Step Process

**Step 1 — Choose a life domain you know well**

Do not start with technology or trends. Start with a domain where you have natural access to people who perform the job: your profession, your community, a problem you yourself face, a sector where you have worked.

Examples of starting points:
- "Small rural producers who sell directly to consumers"
- "Private tutors who manage students and payments"
- "Parents of children with special needs who are looking for therapies"

**Step 2 — Conduct 5 Switch Interviews**

Find 5 people who recently switched solutions (or who would like to switch but haven't yet). Conduct the interview following the 10-step structure. Record with permission. Transcribe or note the moments of Push, Pull, Anxiety and Habit.

After the interviews, look for patterns: which Push appears most often? Which Anxiety prevents the most people from switching?

**Step 3 — Build the Job Map and list Desired Outcomes**

Based on the interview accounts, build the 8-step Job Map for the identified job. For each step, list 3 to 5 desired outcomes — the metrics the interviewees use to know whether they are being successful.

**Step 4 — Calculate the Opportunity Score and identify the gap**

Ask a small sample (even if it is just the same 5 interviewees) to rate each outcome on importance and satisfaction. Calculate the score. Outcomes with a score above 12 indicate where there is real room for a better product or service.

### Key Question for Each Framework

| Framework | Central question for idea generation |
|-----------|--------------------------------------|
| JTBD | "What job are people trying to do that current solutions do poorly?" |
| Switch Interview | "What pushed and attracted my customer — and what held them back? Can I remove the brake?" |
| ODI | "Which part of the job is very important but very poorly served?" |
| Disruption | "Who doesn't consume today due to lack of access, cost or complexity?" |

## Key Takeaways

1. **JTBD redefines competition.** The milkshake did not compete with other milkshakes — it competed with banana and bagel. Understanding the real job reveals unexpected competitors and opportunities that traditional market analyses overlook.

2. **The job always has three layers.** Functional (what), emotional (how it feels) and social (how it is perceived). Products that serve only the functional layer become commoditized. Products that serve all three layers create loyalty.

3. **The Switch Interview is JTBD's most powerful research tool.** Talking about the moment of the switch — and not about the product — reveals the real job, the forces that shape it and the design opportunities that competitors do not see.

4. **ODI quantifies where the opportunity is.** The Opportunity Score turns intuition into data: outcomes with high importance and low satisfaction are the treasure map. There is no need to invent — you need to measure where existing solutions fail.

5. **Disruption is not about technology — it is about trajectory.** An inferior product that serves non-consumers or overserved customers, and that has room to improve, is more threatening than it seems. Incumbents ignore this pattern because their incentives point in the opposite direction.

6. **The three frameworks complement each other.** JTBD identifies the job. ODI prioritizes where to act within the job. Disruption Theory suggests the market entry strategy. Together, they form a complete system of customer-driven innovation.

7. **Personal application starts with 5 interviews.** You do not need a budget, a team or a company to apply JTBD. Five well-conducted Switch Interviews reveal more about a business opportunity than months of secondary market analysis.
