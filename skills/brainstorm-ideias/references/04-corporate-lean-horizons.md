# Brainstorming and Disruptive Ideas - Corporate Innovation: Lean Startup, Three Horizons and Portfolio Frameworks

> Research Date: 2026-04-29
> Angle: Corporate innovation - Lean Startup, Build-Measure-Learn, Three Horizons Model, Innovation Ambition Matrix
> Depth: detailed
> Goal: Apply personally
> Knowledge Level: Basic

## Overview

This document covers the main corporate innovation frameworks aimed at idea portfolio management and the structured validation of new initiatives. The focus is on tools that help both organizations and individuals balance optimizing the present with exploring the future: the Lean Startup Build-Measure-Learn cycle, the Lean Canvas as a one-page ideation tool, McKinsey's Three Horizons model for allocating effort over time, the Innovation Ambition Matrix for visualizing portfolio ambition, Doblin's Ten Types of Innovation for identifying unexplored dimensions, and Rita McGrath's Discovery-Driven Planning for planning under uncertainty.

These frameworks are complementary: the Lean Startup offers the tactical validation process, the Three Horizons and the Ambition Matrix provide the strategic portfolio framing, the Ten Types broaden the view of where to innovate, and Discovery-Driven Planning connects ambition to structured execution. Together, they form a coherent system for anyone who wants to generate, prioritize and test ideas in a disciplined way.

For personal use, these frameworks can be applied to side projects, digital products, career transitions or any initiative where there is uncertainty about the market, the solution or the sustaining model.

## Lean Startup: The Validated Learning Cycle

The Lean Startup is a methodology developed by Eric Ries, published in 2011, for building businesses and products under conditions of extreme uncertainty. The central premise is that a startup's success does not depend on a perfect plan, but on how fast it learns what works and discards what does not. As Ries describes it: "startup success can be engineered by following the process, which means it can be learned, and it can be taught." ([The Lean Startup Principles](https://theleanstartup.com/principles))

### The Build-Measure-Learn Loop

The operational core of the Lean Startup is the Build-Measure-Learn cycle. The goal is not to run the cycle once, but to repeat it quickly to reduce waste and reach a sustainable business model sooner. ([How to Use the Build, Measure, Learn Loop](https://userpilot.com/blog/build-measure-learn/))

**Build:** Turn a hypothesis into a minimum viable product (MVP) as quickly as possible. The MVP is not an incomplete or low-quality product — it is the leanest version capable of testing the main hypothesis with real customers, delivering genuine value to the first adopters. ([The Lean Startup Methodology](https://businessstrategytoolkit.com/blogs/2024-08/lean-startup-methodology-blog/))

**Measure:** Collect data on how customers interact with the MVP. The emphasis is on actionable metrics — those that reveal causality and guide decisions — and not on "vanity metrics" such as number of visits with no correlation to real behavior.

**Learn:** Analyze the data to validate or refute the initial hypotheses. Validated learning is the unit of progress: "the process of demonstrating empirically that a team has discovered valuable truths about a startup's present and future business prospects." ([The Lean Startup Principles](https://theleanstartup.com/principles))

### Validated Learning

The concept of validated learning sets the Lean Startup apart from intuitive approaches. Instead of measuring progress by features delivered or revenue generated, the framework proposes measuring how much the team has learned about what customers really need. This involves formulating explicit hypotheses before building and defining success or failure criteria before measuring.

A classic example cited by Ries is Zappos: before building any e-commerce infrastructure, the founder photographed shoes from physical stores, posted them online and, when someone bought, went to the store in person to buy and deliver them. This approach validated the central hypothesis — customers buy shoes online — at minimal cost. ([The Lean Startup Methodology](https://businessstrategytoolkit.com/blogs/2024-08/lean-startup-methodology-blog/))

### Pivot vs. Persevere

After each learning cycle, the team faces the most critical decision in the Lean Startup: **persevere** (continue in the current direction because the data confirm the hypotheses) or **pivot** (make a structural course correction to test a new fundamental hypothesis about product, strategy or growth engine).

A pivot is not a failure — it is a strategic change based on evidence. Common types of pivot include:

- **Customer segment pivot:** the product solves a real problem, but for a different segment than expected
- **Solution pivot:** the identified problem is real, but the solution needs to change
- **Business model pivot:** product and market are right, but the monetization needs to be reworked
- **Channel pivot:** the product is valid, but the distribution channel needs to change

### Types of MVP

The MVP takes different forms depending on the hypothesis being tested:

| MVP Type | Description | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Concierge MVP | Service delivered manually, without automation | Zappos buying shoes in physical stores |
| Landing Page MVP | Page describing the product before it exists, measuring interest through sign-ups | Dropbox with a demo video |
| Wizard of Oz MVP | Looks automated, but is operated by people behind the scenes | Early Airbnb with manual curation |
| Clickable prototype | Navigable interface without real functionality | App screens to validate UX |
| Single-feature product | Version with only the core functionality | Notes app without sync, without tags |

### Personal Application

For individual use, the Lean Startup is especially useful for testing digital products, content, freelance services or any idea with market uncertainty:

1. Define the main hypothesis: "I believe [segment X] has the problem [Y] and would pay [Z] for a solution"
2. Build the simplest possible MVP to test that hypothesis
3. Measure with specific metrics (conversion rate, retention, willingness to pay)
4. Decide: persevere, pivot or abandon

## Lean Canvas: Business Model on One Page

The Lean Canvas was created by Ash Maurya in 2010 as an adaptation of Alexander Osterwalder's Business Model Canvas for early-stage startups. The central difference: while the BMC maps an existing business holistically, the Lean Canvas focuses on identifying and testing the riskiest hypotheses before building anything. ([Lean Canvas - leancanvas.com](https://leancanvas.com/))

### The 9 Blocks

The Lean Canvas replaces four blocks of the original BMC (Key Partners, Key Activities, Key Resources, Customer Relationships) with blocks more relevant to startups:

| Block | Description | Central Question |
|---|---|---|
| Problem | The top 3 problems the segment faces | What real pain am I solving? |
| Existing Alternatives | How people solve it today | What will I replace? |
| Customer Segments | Who the target customers and early adopters are | For whom exactly? |
| Unique Value Proposition | Why my solution is different and relevant | What makes me unique? |
| Solution | The 3 main features/approaches | How do I solve the problem? |
| Channels | How I reach customers | How do I sell and distribute? |
| Revenue Streams | How I monetize | How do I sustain this? |
| Cost Structure | Main expenses | What does it cost to operate? |
| Key Metrics | Indicators that show whether it is working | How do I know if it is going well? |
| Unfair Advantage | What cannot be easily copied | What protects me? |

([Lean Canvas - Flowup](https://www.flowup.me/blog/lean-canvas/))

### Recommended Filling Order

The filling order does not follow the visual structure of the canvas. Ash Maurya recommends starting with the elements of greatest uncertainty and risk:

1. Customer Segments — who you are serving
2. Problem — what real pain they have
3. Unique Value Proposition — your central promise
4. Solution — how you solve it
5. Channels — how it reaches them
6. Revenue Streams / Cost Structure — viability
7. Key Metrics — how to measure success
8. Unfair Advantage — defensible differentiator

### Simplified Example

Idea: study platform for people who want to learn programming after age 30

```
Problem: Generic courses do not consider the context of adults with little time
           Theoretical content without immediate practical application
           Lack of a community of peers at the same life stage

Segment: Professionals aged 30-45 in a career transition into technology

Unique Value Proposition: Learn programming in 30 min/day with real projects from your current industry

Solution: Tracks adapted by industry (finance, HR, marketing)
          Projects the student already uses in their current job
          Community of peers with a similar profile

Key Metrics: % of students completing module 1, NPS, average session time

Unfair Advantage: Partnerships with companies that sponsor employee training
```

### Lean Canvas vs. Business Model Canvas

The Business Model Canvas is better suited to mapping an existing business or planning a more mature operation. The Lean Canvas is ideal in the ideation and initial validation phase, when the hypotheses still need to be tested. After consistent validation, the Lean Canvas can evolve into a full BMC.

## Three Horizons: Innovation Portfolio Over Time

The Three Horizons model was developed by Mehrdad Baghai, Stephen Coley and David White in 1999, in the book "The Alchemy of Growth", and popularized by McKinsey. The framework solves a structural problem of organizations (and people): the tendency to focus only on the present while neglecting the future, or to chase the future while losing the current base. ([Three Horizons - Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Three_Horizons))

### The Three Horizons

**Horizon 1 (H1) — Core Business**

H1 represents the current business, the products and services that generate most of the revenue and value today. The focus is on optimizing, defending and improving what already exists.

- Timeframe: short term (1-3 years)
- Risk: low
- Objective: maximize current efficiency and profitability
- Corporate example: BIC continuously refining the design and durability of its pens ([Innovation Horizons - Aevo](https://blog.aevo.com.br/horizontes-de-inovacao/))
- Personal example: optimizing your current skills to be more productive and better paid in your present job

**Horizon 2 (H2) — Emerging Businesses**

H2 covers opportunities adjacent to the core business — extensions into new markets, new customer segments or new product lines that leverage existing competencies.

- Timeframe: medium term (2-5 years)
- Risk: moderate
- Objective: scale initiatives with validated potential, build new sources of revenue
- Corporate example: Uber Eats applying the logistics logic of transportation to food delivery ([Innovation Horizons - Aevo](https://blog.aevo.com.br/horizontes-de-inovacao/))
- Personal example: using your current expertise to create a digital product or freelance service in an adjacent area

**Horizon 3 (H3) — Experimentation and Transformation**

H3 is the space of radical innovation, where ideas that may redefine the market or create entirely new categories are explored. There is high uncertainty and low predictability of return.

- Timeframe: long term (5-12 years)
- Risk: high
- Objective: test disruptive hypotheses, create options for the future
- Corporate example: Amazon Go (checkout-free store), Hyperloop ([Innovation Horizons - Aevo](https://blog.aevo.com.br/horizontes-de-inovacao/))
- Personal example: exploring a completely new area that may become relevant 5-10 years from now

### The Three Horizons Operate Simultaneously

The most common mistake in applying the model is treating the horizons as sequential phases — finishing H1 to start H2 and then H3. The framework proposes that the three horizons should be worked in parallel, with different levels of energy, resources and management tools. ([What is the 3 horizons model - Board of Innovation](https://www.boardofinnovation.com/blog/what-is-the-3-horizons-model-how-can-you-use-it/))

### Resource Distribution

The recommended allocation — used as a reference by McKinsey and mentioned by Eric Schmidt when describing Google — is:

| Horizon | Resource Allocation | Focus |
|---|---|---|
| H1 | 70% | Protect and optimize the current business |
| H2 | 20% | Scale adjacent opportunities |
| H3 | 10% | Explore transformational innovations |

This distribution is not a rigid rule, but a heuristic to avoid two extremes: neglecting the present (70% in H3) or never investing in the future (100% in H1).

### Warning About the Original Model

An influential 2019 HBR article warned that the original model assumed a temporal progression that no longer matches the reality of digital innovation, where startups can reach the H3 of large companies in months. The model remains useful as a communication and portfolio planning tool, but should be interpreted with flexibility regarding timeframes. ([McKinsey's Three Horizons Model - HBR](https://hbr.org/2019/02/mckinseys-three-horizons-model-defined-innovation-for-years-heres-why-it-no-longer-applies))

### Personal Application

To manage a personal portfolio of projects and initiatives:

```
H1 (my current job):
  - Improve performance in the main role
  - Deepen core skills
  - Build reputation and network in the current industry

H2 (adjacent expansion):
  - Create content about my area of expertise
  - Offer consulting/freelance services to companies in other industries
  - Learn skills complementary to the current ones

H3 (long-term bets):
  - Explore a new area that may be relevant in 5 years
  - Build a digital product experimentally
  - Invest in learning emerging technologies
```

## Innovation Ambition Matrix: Where and How to Compete

The Innovation Ambition Matrix was developed by Bansi Nagji and Geoff Tuff and published in the Harvard Business Review in 2012 in the article "Managing Your Innovation Portfolio". The framework is an evolution of the Three Horizons concept, adding a spatial dimension: not only *when* to innovate, but *where* to compete (markets) and *how* to compete (products/solutions). ([Innovation Ambition Matrix - HBR](https://hbr.org/2012/05/a-simple-tool-you-need-to-mana))

### The Two Axes

The matrix organizes innovation initiatives along two axes:

- **Horizontal axis (How to compete):** how new the products or solutions are — from incremental improvements to completely new creations
- **Vertical axis (Where to compete):** how new the markets are — from existing customers to markets that do not yet exist

The combination of the two axes generates three zones of ambition:

### The Three Zones

**Core (Incremental)**

Innovations that optimize existing products and services for markets and customers that are already known. They use the assets, competencies and processes already available.

- Low risk, predictable and short-term return
- Generates most of the current revenue
- Examples: new flavors of an existing product, interface improvement of an app that is already in use

**Adjacent (Expansion)**

Innovations that take the company into new territories without completely abandoning what it already knows how to do. It can be an existing product for a new segment, or a new product for current customers.

- Moderate risk, medium-term return
- Leverages existing competencies in a new way
- Examples: a B2B software company launching a B2C version; a traditional bank creating a digital account

**Transformational (Disruptive)**

Radical innovations for markets that do not yet exist or are completely new to the organization. They require new competencies, new business models and tolerance for high uncertainty.

- High risk, potential for exponential return in the long term
- Often led by separate teams or internal startups
- Examples: Google Maps when launched (new market, new product), Tesla reinventing the automotive industry

([What is the ambition matrix - Idea to Value](https://www.ideatovalue.com/inno/nickskillicorn/2021/01/what-is-the-ambition-matrix-and-how-does-it-work-as-part-of-an-innovation-portfolio/))

### The 70-20-10 Rule

Nagji and Tuff identified, from an empirical analysis of high-performing companies, a distribution of effort that tends to maximize total innovation return:

| Zone | Effort Distribution | Contribution to Revenue |
|---|---|---|
| Core | 70% | ~10% (incremental return) |
| Adjacent | 20% | ~20% |
| Transformational | 10% | ~70% (in the long term, if successful) |

The counterintuitive logic is that transformational innovations, although they receive only 10% of the resources, have the greatest value-creation potential when successful. The imbalance between effort and return reinforces the importance of always maintaining some level of investment in H3/transformational. ([Innovation Ambition Matrix - MBA Knowledge Base](https://mbaknol.com/strategic-management/the-innovation-ambition-matrix/))

### Difference Between the Ambition Matrix and Three Horizons

| Dimension | Three Horizons | Innovation Ambition Matrix |
|---|---|---|
| Main focus | Time and resource management | Portfolio and ambition management |
| Axes | Time x Value generated | Product novelty x Market novelty |
| Central question | When to invest in each type? | Where and how to compete? |
| Best use | Temporal innovation roadmap | Diagnosis of the current portfolio |

### Practical Application

A useful exercise: list all current projects or ideas and classify each one into the three zones. If most are concentrated in Core, the portfolio is under-investing in the future. If everything is in Transformational without a solid Core base, there is a sustainability risk.

## Ten Types of Innovation (Doblin / Larry Keeley)

The Ten Types of Innovation framework was developed by Larry Keeley and his colleagues at the consultancy Doblin over decades of analyzing successful innovations, and consolidated in the book "Ten Types of Innovation: The Discipline of Building Breakthroughs" (2013). The central finding: lasting innovations rarely come from product improvements alone. Companies that combine multiple types of innovation simultaneously create advantages that are harder to copy. ([Ten Types of Innovation - Doblin](https://doblin.com/ten-types))

### The Three Groups and the 10 Types

**Group 1: Configuration (how the business works)**

Innovations in the company's internal structure, financial model and operations.

| Type | Description | Example |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Profit Model | How the company generates revenue and captures value | Monthly subscription instead of one-time purchase (Netflix) |
| 2. Network | Partnerships and connections with other organizations | Apple's App Store connecting developers and users |
| 3. Structure | Organization of talent, assets and competencies | Spotify with autonomous squads per product |
| 4. Process | Proprietary methods of production or delivery | Toyota's lean production (Toyota Production System) |

**Group 2: Offering (what is delivered)**

Innovations in the product or service itself.

| Type | Description | Example |
|---|---|---|
| 5. Product Performance | Product quality, features and capabilities | Apple Silicon processors with superior performance |
| 6. Product System | How products connect and complement each other | Apple ecosystem (iPhone + Watch + AirPods + iCloud) |

**Group 3: Experience (how customers perceive and interact)**

Innovations in the way customers discover, use and relate to the product.

| Type | Description | Example |
|---|---|---|
| 7. Service | Support, ease of use and perceived value | Amazon's 24-hour support with no-questions-asked returns |
| 8. Channel | How the product reaches the customer | Direct-to-consumer sales without intermediaries (Tesla) |
| 9. Brand | Representation of values that resonate with customers | Patagonia with a brand tied to radical sustainability |
| 10. Customer Engagement | Purchase experience and ongoing relationship | Starbucks loyalty programs with gamification |

([The Ten Types of Innovation Framework - Hype Innovation](https://www.hypeinnovation.com/blog/using-the-ten-types-of-innovation-framework))

### Why This Matters

Doblin's research showed that:

- Top innovators combine, on average, twice as many types of innovation as the industry average
- Innovating only in Product Performance (type 5) is the most common and the most easily copied
- Combinations that cut across the three groups (Configuration + Offering + Experience) create more sustainable competitive advantages

Doblin recommends using at least 5 of the 10 types simultaneously, ensuring presence in all three groups. ([Ten Types of Innovation - ModelThinkers](https://modelthinkers.com/mental-model/ten-types-of-innovation))

### Application to Ideation

Use the 10 types as an opportunity checklist: when analyzing an existing product or service, systematically ask "are we innovating in this dimension?". Missing dimensions represent unexplored opportunities.

Example applied to an online tutoring service:
- Profit Model: charge for results (passing the exam) instead of per hour
- Network: connect teachers to schools as distribution partners
- Process: content personalization algorithm by learning style
- Service: money-back guarantee if the student does not pass the exam
- Engagement: gamified track with achievements visible to parents

## Discovery-Driven Planning: Planning Under Uncertainty

Discovery-Driven Planning (DDP) was developed by Rita Gunther McGrath and Ian MacMillan, originally published in the Harvard Business Review in 1995. The framework emerged from observing a consistent pattern in failed projects: planning based on untested assumptions, excessive funding right at the start, and few opportunities to redirect when new data emerged. ([Discovery-Driven Planning - HBR 1995](https://hbr.org/1995/07/discovery-driven-planning))

### The Problem with Conventional Planning

Conventional planning assumes that the future is predictable enough to be detailed. For new projects and innovations, this is a fallacy: the assumptions that support the plan are often untested and may be wrong. The result is growing investment in a potentially mistaken path, with no structured checkpoints for review.

DDP proposes the inversion: instead of starting from implicit assumptions and projecting results, make all assumptions explicit and create systematic mechanisms to test them throughout the process. ([Discovery-Driven Planning - Customer IQ](https://www.getcustomeriq.com/blog/discovery-driven-planning-product-strategy-framework-explained))

### The Four Central Elements

**1. Identifying and Mapping Assumptions**

The first step is to explicitly separate what is known for certain from what is being assumed. Each assumption must be listed and prioritized by its importance to the project's success and by its uncertainty (the less that is known, the more critical it is to test).

Guiding questions:
- What do we know for certain about this market?
- What are we assuming about customer behavior?
- What are we assuming about our delivery capability?
- Which assumption, if false, would make the project stop making sense?

**2. Reverse Income Statement**

Instead of optimistically projecting revenues and costs and seeing whether the result is positive, DDP inverts the logic:

1. Define what minimum return justifies the investment (e.g., a 20% margin on R$ 2M of revenue)
2. Calculate the revenue needed to reach that return
3. Determine how many customers, at what average price, with what conversion rate, are needed
4. Ask: "Is it realistic to reach these numbers? Which assumptions would need to be true?"

This approach makes explicit what needs to be true for the project to make sense, before any significant investment. ([A Refresher on Discovery-Driven Planning - HBR](https://hbr.org/2017/02/a-refresher-on-discovery-driven-planning))

**3. Checkpoints and Decision Milestones**

Instead of periodic progress reviews (which tend to justify prior investments), DDP defines specific milestones at which critical assumptions will be tested. Each milestone is a question, not a deliverable:

- Milestone 1: "Would customers pay for the product before it exists?" (e.g., pre-sale campaign)
- Milestone 2: "Is the real CAC within the assumed range?"
- Milestone 3: "Does the month-3 retention rate support the projected LTV?"

If the answer at any milestone invalidates a critical assumption, the project can be pivoted or ended with controlled loss — instead of continuing to a costlier failure.

**4. Learning Culture**

DDP works only in environments that treat assumption failures as valuable data, not as personal failures. Retrospectives after each milestone, sharing of learnings and openness to revising assumptions are essential cultural components.

### Application Example

Project: launch an online personal finance course for young people aged 20-30

```
Main assumptions to test:
  P1: Young people aged 20-30 perceive financial education as a priority
  P2: They would pay R$ 300 for an 8-week course
  P3: A 40% completion rate generates enough referrals for organic growth
  P4: CAC via Instagram below R$ 80

Reverse Income Statement:
  Goal: R$ 50,000 in revenue in the first semester
  Price: R$ 300 per student
  Students needed: ~167
  Landing page conversion rate: 3%
  Visitors needed: ~5,600
  Estimated cost (CAC R$ 80): R$ 13,360
  Estimated margin: ~73%

  Critical assumption: Is a CAC of R$ 80 achievable with the initial budget?

Milestone 1 (week 2): Landing page with pre-sale. Goal: 10 pre-enrollments at R$ 300.
Milestone 2 (week 6): First module delivered. Goal: NPS > 50, completion rate > 60%.
Milestone 3 (month 3): Check real CAC. If > R$ 150, revise channel or price.
```

### DDP vs. Lean Startup

The two frameworks are complementary and often used together:

| Dimension | Lean Startup | Discovery-Driven Planning |
|---|---|---|
| Origin | Digital startups | Corporate innovation and large projects |
| Cycle | Iterative Build-Measure-Learn | Decision milestones with explicit assumptions |
| MVP | Central — build something fast | Optional — can test assumptions without building |
| Focus | Speed of learning | Making assumptions explicit and testing them |
| Best for | Digital products with short cycles | Projects with more uncertainty and larger investment |

## Framework Comparison

| Framework | Creator | Best For | Central Question |
|---|---|---|---|
| Lean Startup | Eric Ries (2011) | Quickly validating product/market | What do customers really want? |
| Lean Canvas | Ash Maurya (2010) | Structuring and communicating an idea on 1 page | What is the lean business model? |
| Three Horizons | McKinsey / Baghai (1999) | Balancing a portfolio of initiatives over time | Where to invest effort now vs. in the future? |
| Innovation Ambition Matrix | Nagji & Tuff (2012) | Diagnosing and balancing the innovation portfolio | Are we innovating where and as much as we should? |
| Ten Types of Innovation | Doblin / Keeley (2013) | Identifying unexplored dimensions of innovation | In which types of innovation are we blind? |
| Discovery-Driven Planning | Rita McGrath (1995) | Planning projects with high uncertainty | Which assumptions need to be true? |

## Key Takeaways

1. **The Lean Startup is a learning system, not a building system.** The speed of the Build-Measure-Learn cycle matters more than the quality of the initial MVP. The goal is to invalidate wrong hypotheses as early and as cheaply as possible.

2. **The Lean Canvas makes implicit assumptions explicit.** By forcing the articulation of problem, segment, solution and metrics on one page, it reveals inconsistencies and gaps that a traditional business plan obscures.

3. **The Three Horizons must coexist, not follow one another.** Focusing only on H1 secures the present but compromises the future; focusing only on H3 abandons the base. The 70-20-10 distribution is a valuable heuristic for personal and corporate portfolios.

4. **The Innovation Ambition Matrix reveals portfolio imbalances.** Most organizations (and people) are over-invested in Core and under-invested in Transformational. Visualizing the portfolio on the matrix makes this imbalance evident.

5. **Innovating only in the product is the most fragile strategy.** The Ten Types of Innovation show that the most lasting innovations combine multiple dimensions — business model, process, experience — not just product features.

6. **Discovery-Driven Planning protects against the "sunk cost fallacy".** By defining decision milestones with explicit assumptions before investing, it creates a structured mechanism for ending projects that do not validate their central hypotheses.

7. **For personal use, these frameworks scale.** The Lean Canvas can structure a freelance project. The Three Horizons can organize a personal career portfolio. DDP can guide the decision to launch a digital product. None of them requires an organization to be useful.
