# Brainstorming and Disruptive Ideas - Disruptive Techniques: Analogy, Biomimicry and Combination

> Research Date: 2026-04-29
> Angle: Disruptive techniques - Distant analogy, biomimicry, cross-industry transfer, random combination
> Depth: detailed
> Goal: Personal use
> Knowledge Level: Basic

## Overview

This document covers the techniques for generating disruptive ideas that work through transposition: taking the logic, structure or mechanism of a completely different domain and applying it to the problem at hand. These approaches break linear thinking because they force the brain off its habitual tracks of association.

The techniques gathered here share a common principle: innovation rarely comes from nothing. It arises from the combination or creative transplanting of solutions that already exist in other contexts. Steven Johnson, in "Where Good Ideas Come From", describes this as "the adjacent possible" — the frontier of new combinations that becomes accessible from what already exists. The innovator's role is to consciously widen that frontier by crossing domains that do not normally talk to each other.

For individual use, these techniques are especially valuable because they require no team, budget or laboratory. They require disciplined observation, cross-disciplinary curiosity and a willingness to look naive in the face of a problem.

## Biomimicry

### What it is

Biomimicry is the practice of observing the solutions that nature has developed over 3.8 billion years of evolution and translating them to human challenges. The term was popularized by the American biologist Janine Benyus in her book "Biomimicry: Innovation Inspired by Nature" (1997). Benyus defines biomimicry on three levels: imitating natural forms, imitating natural processes and imitating entire ecosystem systems.

In 2008, the Biomimicry Institute launched AskNature.org, an online encyclopedia of nature's solutions catalogued by function — the largest biomimicry database in the world, freely accessible.

### Why it works

Nature does not design for aesthetics: every form, structure and behavior that survived natural selection is an efficient answer to a real problem. The kingfisher (Portuguese: martim-pescador) dives from the dry surface into dense water without losing speed because its beak distributes pressure gradually — it is a 50-million-year-old engineering solution. The termite keeps its colonies at 31°C in environments that vary from 3°C to 42°C with no mechanical equipment at all.

### Classic examples with technical detail

| Human problem | Nature's solution | Result |
|---------------|-------------------|--------|
| Bullet trains caused a sonic boom when leaving tunnels | Kingfisher beak (diameter increases gradually from the tip to the base, distributing water pressure) | Shinkansen train 10% faster and using 15% less energy |
| Commercial building in Harare, Zimbabwe, with no viable air conditioning | Passive ventilation system of termite mounds (central chimney connected to underground tunnels) | Eastgate Centre uses passive ventilation, drastically reducing energy consumption |
| Textile fasteners that would not wear out | Microscopic hooks on burdock (Portuguese: bardana) fruits that cling to any fabric | Velcro, invented by George de Mestral in 1941 after observation under a microscope |

### Step-by-step method for individual use

**Step 1 — Reframe the problem as a function**
Translate the product/service problem into a functional statement. Instead of "I want to make a stronger adhesive", ask: "how does nature hold two surfaces together without permanent glue?". Instead of "how do I reduce climate-control costs?", ask: "how do living organisms regulate temperature without external energy?".

**Step 2 — Research AskNature**
Go to [asknature.org](https://asknature.org) and search for the function identified in the previous step. The site categorizes biological solutions by function (move, protect, regulate, structure, etc.). Read at least 5 entries about different organisms.

**Step 3 — Extract the principle, not the form**
Do not copy the appearance; extract the mechanism. Velcro is not made of burdock — it uses the principle of microscopic hooks in loops. The Shinkansen has no feathers — it uses the hydrodynamic profile of the beak.

**Step 4 — Map it to your domain**
Ask: "if my product/service worked like this organism, what would change?" Write at least 3 hypotheses, even the ones that seem absurd.

**Step 5 — Filter for feasibility**
Of the hypotheses generated, identify which ones are achievable with the resources available. Test the simplest one first.

## Analogical Thinking: Near Analogy vs. Distant Analogy

### What it is

Analogy is the cognitive mechanism of transferring the relational structure of a known situation to a new situation. Innovation by analogy does not work through surface similarity (two things that look alike), but through deep structural mapping: two domains that share the same logic of relationships between parts, even though they are completely different in appearance.

MIT Sloan Management Review distinguishes two types with radically different impact:

- **Near analogy:** same industry or adjacent field. Useful for incremental improvement, hardly disruptive.
- **Distant analogy:** fields with no apparent relation. High disruptive potential because it forces a departure from established mental patterns.

### Why distance matters

Cognition research confirms that solving distant analogies activates brain regions associated with creativity and insight, and produces more structurally rich answers than near analogies. The study published in ScienceDirect "Cross-domain analogical reasoning ability links functional connectome to creativity" confirms that the ability to reason by analogy across domains is one of the best predictors of creativity.

### The canonical example: Ford and the meatpacking plant

Henry Ford did not invent the assembly line from scratch. Visiting the Swift & Company meatpacking plants in Chicago, the engineer William "Pa" Klann observed that workers on the carcass "disassembly line" stayed fixed in position while the product moved. The structure was: moving product, static operator, repetitive specialized task. Klann transported exactly that logical structure to automobile production. The result cut the manufacturing time of a Model T from more than 12 hours to 1 hour and 33 minutes.

The analogy was not about meat or cars — it was about the principle of dividing a complex task into sequenced unit operations with the object in motion.

### The contemporary example: the emergency room and the Formula 1 pit stop

The Grace Omersfield Street Hospital studied Formula 1 pit crews to improve patient handoffs between teams. The two domains are structurally analogous: a high-pressure environment, multiple specialists acting in parallel, critical timing, communication with no margin for error. Applying the model reduced technical errors by 42% and information omissions by 49%.

### Step-by-step method for individual use

**Step 1 — Describe the problem in terms of structure**
Write the logic of the problem in abstract language. Example: "I need to keep quality constant while increasing production speed". Do not mention the sector or product.

**Step 2 — Identify distant domains with the same structural challenge**
Ask: "Who else faces exactly this trade-off?" Think about sports, the military, nature, art, medicine, cooking, aviation. List at least 5 fields that seem irrelevant.

**Step 3 — Study how the distant field solves the problem**
Read, watch or interview. Document the mechanism in detail, without filtering out what seems inapplicable.

**Step 4 — Do the structural mapping**
Build an equivalence table: what is the "pit crew" in your context? What is the "car" in your process? What is the "stop time"? Map each element.

**Step 5 — Generate transfer hypotheses**
For each mapped element, write a hypothesis of how the principle could be applied. Refine later.

## Cross-Industry Transfer

### What it is

Cross-industry transfer is the deliberate practice of seeking solutions already tested in other industries for problems that are unsolved in your own area. It is a systematized form of distant analogy, but with a specific focus on already proven innovations — not on abstract principles, but on concrete practices.

The difference from pure analogy: in distant analogy, you start from a problem and look for similar structures. In cross-industry transfer, you can also start from a solution from another sector and ask: "where could I apply this?"

### Historical examples

- **Assembly line → other industries:** After Ford, the logic of the assembly line spread to household appliances, electronics, textiles and food.
- **Military logistics → civilian supply chain:** Inventory management techniques developed by the US Army in World War II were the basis for what would become supply chain management.
- **F1 pit stop → surgery and hospital logistics:** Multiple hospitals around the world applied pit crew protocols to surgeries and patient transfers.

### Step-by-step method for individual use

**Step 1 — Map the core problem**
Write in one sentence: what is the core problem you cannot solve with the tools of your sector?

**Step 2 — List sectors with extreme operating characteristics**
Sectors that operate with zero tolerance for error (aviation, emergency medicine, the military, high-performance sports) and sectors with massive scale (logistics, fast food, entertainment) tend to have highly refined solutions for common problems.

**Step 3 — Research how those sectors solve the same problem**
Use searches such as: "[problem] how aviation solves it" or "[challenge] military management". Read cases, not just theory.

**Step 4 — Identify the transferable principle**
Extract the mechanism, not the artifact. Aviation's "pre-flight checklists" → principle: mandatory sequential verification before a critical action → applicable to product launches, consulting engagements, surgeries, etc.

**Step 5 — Prototype the transfer**
Adapt the principle to your context with the smallest possible changes. Test on a small scale before scaling up.

## Random Word / Random Image (Random Input)

### What it is

The Random Input technique is a lateral thinking tool developed by Edward de Bono. It consists of introducing a completely random element — a word, image, object or sound — and forcing connections between that element and the problem at hand.

The counterintuitive logic is that randomness is not noise: it diverts the brain from its habitual neural paths of association and forces the exploration of patterns that would normally be ignored. De Bono illustrates this with the random word "nose" applied to the problem of a photocopier: the association led to the idea that the machine could emit a lavender scent when the paper ran out — a sensory form of alert.

### Why it works

When the brain tries to connect two unrelated elements, it has to look for relational structures at a more abstract level. This forced-connection search process is exactly the mechanism that generates creative insights — because it passes through mental territories that direct reasoning avoids.

### Variations of the technique

- **Random Word:** a word drawn at random from a dictionary or an online generator
- **Random Image:** a random image from a photo bank or a magazine opened at random
- **Random Object:** a physical object from the immediate surroundings chosen at random
- **SCAMPER:** a structured variation that uses a checklist of transformations (Substitute, Combine, Adapt, Modify, Put to other uses, Eliminate, Reverse) applied to the random element

### Step-by-step method for individual use

**Step 1 — Define the problem clearly**
Write the problem in one objective sentence. Example: "How can I make customer onboarding more memorable?"

**Step 2 — Select the random element**
Options: open a dictionary to any page and use the first word. Open Google Images, search for a random letter and use the first image. Use an online random word generator.

**Step 3 — List attributes of the random element**
If the word is "ocean", list: deep, salty, currents, waves, less than 20% explored, surface vs. depths themes, increasing pressure, etc.

**Step 4 — Force connections with the problem**
For each attribute listed, ask: "how could this apply to my problem?" Write down all the connections, even the absurd ones. "Depth" → does onboarding have layers that the customer discovers progressively? "Less than 20% explored" → most customers use only 20% of the features — what if onboarding revealed the product's "depths"?

**Step 5 — Filter and develop**
Of the connections generated, select 2 or 3 that look promising. Develop each one into a testable hypothesis.

## The Medici Effect and the Intersection of Fields

### What it is

Frans Johansson, in "The Medici Effect" (2004), argues that the most disruptive ideas emerge at the "Intersection" — the point where different disciplines, cultures and industries meet and collide. The name comes from the Medici family, who in the 14th century financed artists, philosophers, scientists and architects in the same city, generating the intellectual density that produced the Renaissance.

The central thesis: when you combine concepts from two different fields, the number of possible new ideas grows exponentially — not linearly. Each field has hundreds of concepts; each combination between fields generates a new class of possibilities.

### Directional ideas vs. intersectional ideas

Johansson distinguishes two types of innovation:

| Type | Description | Example |
|------|-------------|---------|
| **Directional** | Incremental improvement within a field | More efficient car engine |
| **Intersectional** | Combination of distinct fields | GPS + cell phone + maps → real-time navigation |

Intersectional ideas have greater disruptive potential because they do not exist in the imagination of those who are inside just one of the fields.

### Three forces that create intersections

Johansson identifies historical forces that widen the opportunities for intersection:

1. **Movement of people:** globalization and mobility bring diverse perspectives together.
2. **Scientific convergence:** computational biology, neuroeconomics, parametric architecture — disciplines that once did not talk to each other now overlap.
3. **Computing:** digital tools make it possible to combine domains that once required separate specialists.

### Associative barriers

The main obstacle to intersectional innovation is associative barriers — the mental patterns that make us automatically connect concepts within the same field and prevent us from seeing connections outside it. Johansson suggests concrete practices to lower these barriers:

- Expose yourself to multiple cultures (travel, reading outside your area, spending time with people from radically different backgrounds)
- Learn languages (demonstrably increases cognitive flexibility)
- Reverse assumptions (take the central premise of your field and ask: what if the opposite were true?)
- Adopt multiple perspectives on the same problem (how would a biologist see this? and a chef?)

### Step-by-step method for individual use

**Step 1 — Map your fields of knowledge**
List all the domains you know at least minimally: your profession, hobbies, reading, experiences. Include even skills that seem irrelevant.

**Step 2 — Identify radically distinct fields that attract you**
Choose 2 or 3 fields outside your main area that arouse genuine curiosity. Curiosity matters because you will need to invest time in them.

**Step 3 — Study the chosen fields in enough depth**
You do not need to become an expert, but you need to understand the mechanisms, the open problems and the recent solutions. One book or 10 articles from each field is usually enough to get started.

**Step 4 — Create pairs of concepts across fields**
Take a central concept from your field and a central concept from the distant field. Ask: what happens when these two concepts meet? Write 5 different pairs.

**Step 5 — Generate quantity before quality**
Johansson emphasizes that the quality of ideas emerges from quantity. Generate at least 20 combinations before evaluating any of them. The goal of the first session is never to evaluate — only to generate.

## Combinatorial Creativity and the Adjacent Possible

### What it is

Steven Johnson, in "Where Good Ideas Come From" (2010), proposes that innovation is not invention from scratch — it is the combination of existing elements in new ways. He uses the concept of the "adjacent possible" (coined by the biologist Stuart Kauffman to describe evolution) to explain why great ideas arise in historical clusters and never ahead of their time.

The adjacent possible is the frontier of combinations that become accessible from the current state of knowledge. Each combination realized opens up new combinations that were previously impossible — like doors that reveal other doors. This explains why the telephone, the jet engine and calculus were invented independently and simultaneously by different people: all were on the adjacent frontier of the knowledge of the time.

### Practical implication for innovation

The direct consequence: to generate disruptive ideas, you need to widen the frontier of your personal adjacent possible. This is done by consuming knowledge from diverse fields, maintaining "liquid networks" of contacts from different areas and cultivating the habit of noting down incomplete ideas that may connect with others in the future.

Johnson also identifies that good ideas rarely arrive ready-made — they arrive as "half ideas" that need other fragments to be completed. The habit of keeping an idea journal (he calls it a "commonplace book") is a way of creating a critical mass of fragments that can combine.

### Einstein's combinatorial play

A practice attributed to Einstein and cited by Johnson is "combinatorial play": deliberately putting concepts from different fields in mental contact, with no immediate goal, just to observe what emerges. Einstein did this visually, imagining impossible physical scenarios (riding a beam of light) before formulating any equation.

For individual use, this translates to: set aside 15 to 30 minutes a week to "play" with two concepts from different fields with no agenda for results. Write down what emerges, even if it seems meaningless.

### The Mash-up technique

Mash-up is an explicit form of combinatorial creativity: taking two existing products, services, processes or concepts and deliberately fusing them to generate a third. GPS + camera + phone = smartphone is the most impactful example of recent history.

**Method:**

1. List 10 products or services from your sector.
2. List 10 products or services from completely different sectors.
3. Create a 10x10 grid of combinations.
4. For each combination, write in one sentence what the resulting product/service would be.
5. Select the 5 most promising combinations and develop them into testable hypotheses.

## Generative Metaphor

### What it is

Donald Schön, in "Generative Metaphor: A Perspective on Problem-Setting in Social Policy" (1993), proposes that the way we frame a problem determines the solutions we are able to see. A generative metaphor does not describe a problem — it rebuilds it in a way that opens new solution spaces.

The classic example: researchers were trying to create a paintbrush with synthetic bristles. They were stuck because they thought of the problem as "how do we make synthetic bristles work like natural bristles?" An engineer said: "a paintbrush is a kind of pump!" That metaphor reconfigured the problem — from "materials that imitate hair" to "a mechanism that moves fluid in a controlled way" — and opened up a whole new class of solutions.

### Why it works

The generative metaphor does not solve the problem — it redefines it. By naming a problem with a metaphor from another domain, you automatically inherit all the solutions and conceptual tools of that domain. The problem changes shape and, with it, so do the possible solutions.

### Examples of generative metaphors

| Original metaphor | Generative metaphor | Impact |
|-------------------|---------------------|--------|
| "A city is a place of habitation" | "A city is a living organism" | Led to systemic urban planning, infrastructure as metabolism |
| "Software is a manufactured product" | "Software is a garden" | Led to agile practices: iterative cultivation, pruning, organic growth |
| "A paintbrush has bristles" | "A paintbrush is a pump" | Opened up solutions based on fluid mechanics |
| "A patient is treated by a doctor" | "Health is managed like a supply chain" | Led to preventive medicine and telemedicine at scale |

### Step-by-step method for individual use

**Step 1 — Write down the current implicit metaphor**
What metaphor does your sector use to describe the problem? It is usually implicit in the language. "Capturing customers" uses a hunting or fishing metaphor. "Cultivating relationships" uses a gardening metaphor. Identify the dominant metaphor.

**Step 2 — Identify what the current metaphor blocks**
Which solutions are impossible within the current metaphor? Hunting implies scarcity and competition; gardening implies patience and nurturing. What does each metaphor make unthinkable?

**Step 3 — Generate alternative metaphors from distant fields**
Ask: "what if the problem were [random domain]?" Try at least 5 metaphors from completely different fields: biology, physics, cooking, music, architecture, sports, theater.

**Step 4 — Explore each metaphor for its implications**
For each alternative metaphor, ask: what concepts does it bring? What solutions does it suggest? What does it make possible that the current metaphor made impossible?

**Step 5 — Select the most generative metaphor**
Not the prettiest or most intuitive — the one that opens the most new solution space. Develop the implications of that metaphor into concrete hypotheses.

## Recommended Sequence for Individual Use

For those who are starting to apply these techniques on their own, the order below maximizes progressive learning:

1. **Start with Random Word** — it is the quickest entry point and requires no prior research. Use it on any everyday problem to train the forced-connection muscle.

2. **Move on to Generative Metaphor** — after a few exercises with randomness, try deliberately reframing a problem with metaphors from other fields.

3. **Apply Distant Analogy** — with the reframing skill developed, start systematically searching for who else solves your type of problem in distant fields.

4. **Try Cross-Industry Transfer** — read regularly outside your area. Set aside 30 minutes a week to read about a sector completely different from your own.

5. **Practice Biomimicry** — use AskNature.org for at least one problem per month. The exercise of translating problems into functional language is valuable in itself.

6. **Cultivate the Medici Effect** — in the long run, the most important investment is deliberately widening your circle of fields of interest. Read one book per quarter from an area completely different from your own.

## Key Points

1. Disruptive innovation rarely arises from inside a field — it emerges from importing logic from external domains. All the techniques in this document are variations of that principle.
2. The distance between domains matters: near analogies generate incremental improvements; distant analogies generate breakthroughs. Calibrate the distance according to the result you are seeking.
3. Biomimicry offers the largest database of tested solutions available: 3.8 billion years of evolution. The practical entry point is AskNature.org, free and organized by function.
4. Random Word is not a game — it is a formal technique with a solid cognitive basis. Randomness forces a departure from habitual associative patterns.
5. Schön's generative metaphor is the most powerful technique for redefining problems that seem unsolvable: changing the metaphor changes the solution space.
6. Steven Johnson confirms: good ideas are combinations of existing elements. Widening your personal adjacent possible requires deliberate and diversified consumption of knowledge.
7. For individual use, the most important discipline is record-keeping: note down incomplete ideas, improbable connections and metaphors that arise. The combination of fragments happens over time, not necessarily in a single session.
