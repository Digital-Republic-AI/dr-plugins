# Brainstorm and Disruptive Ideas - Classic Frameworks: Design Thinking and Human-Centered Methods

> Research Date: 2026-04-29
> Angle: Classic frameworks - Design Thinking and human-centered methods (IDEO, Stanford d.school, Double Diamond)
> Depth: detailed
> Goal: Personal application
> Knowledge Level: Basic

## Overview

Design Thinking is a human-centered problem-solving approach that places people's needs, behaviors and experiences at the center of the entire solution design process. Popularized by the IDEO consultancy and systematized by the Stanford d.school, the method combines deep empathy with expansive ideation and rapid prototyping, allowing anyone — not only professional designers — to develop innovative solutions to real problems.

What sets Design Thinking apart from other innovation methodologies is its emphasis on questioning the very definition of the problem before generating solutions. While traditional approaches start from a fixed brief and look for the best answer to it, Design Thinking holds that the wrong framing of the problem is the main cause of solutions that do not work. For this reason, the empathy and definition phases receive as much attention as ideation itself.

This document covers three interlinked frameworks that derive from the Design Thinking tradition: the Stanford d.school 5-phase process (Empathize, Define, Ideate, Prototype, Test), the British Design Council's Double Diamond (Discover, Define, Develop, Deliver) and the 5-day Design Sprint by Jake Knapp and Google Ventures. For each framework, the practical tools, the internal ideation techniques and guidelines for individual use are described.

## The 5 Phases of Design Thinking (Stanford d.school)

The 5-phase model was systematized by the Stanford d.school and is the most widespread Design Thinking reference in the world. The phases are not linear, strictly sequential steps: teams and individuals often return to earlier phases when they obtain new information, run phases in parallel, or repeat cycles until they reach satisfactory solutions. The model should be understood as a set of **modes of thinking**, not as a rigid checklist ([The 5 Stages of Design Thinking | IxDF](https://ixdf.org/literature/article/5-stages-in-the-design-thinking-process)).

### Phase 1 — Empathize

**Objective:** Develop a deep understanding of the people for whom a solution is being created, setting aside personal assumptions about the problem.

Empathizing means observing, engaging and immersing. It is not enough to ask people what they want — you need to understand how they think, what they feel, what they do and why they do it. Most solutions fail because they start from incorrect assumptions about users. This phase exists to replace assumptions with evidence.

**Practical techniques for individual use:**

- **Empathy interviews:** Open conversations of 30 to 60 minutes with people affected by the problem. Use open questions ("Tell me about the last time you...") instead of closed questions ("Do you prefer A or B?"). Record on audio or in dense notes.
- **Direct observation (shadowing):** Accompany the person performing the task in their real environment. What people do often differs from what they say they do.
- **User diary:** Ask the person to document their experience over several days, in text or photos, capturing moments that a single interview would not reach.
- **Autoethnography:** When the designer is also the user, document your own experience with analytical distance.

**For individual use without a team:** Conduct at least 3 interviews with different people who live the problem. The diversity of perspectives is more valuable than the number of interviews.

### Phase 2 — Define

**Objective:** Synthesize the observations from the empathy phase into a clear, user-oriented and actionable problem statement.

This phase turns raw research data into creative direction. A well-defined problem is the foundation of all subsequent ideation. The common temptation is to skip this phase and start generating solutions immediately — which usually results in solutions to the wrong problem.

**Tool: Empathy Map**

The empathy map is a visualization that organizes knowledge about a specific user into four quadrants ([Empathy Mapping: The First Step in Design Thinking | NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/empathy-mapping/)):

| Quadrant | Content | What it captures |
|----------|---------|------------------|
| **Says** | Direct quotes from the research | What the user verbalizes |
| **Thinks** | Internal thoughts, including those not verbalized | Concerns, doubts, self-criticism |
| **Does** | Observed physical actions | Real vs. stated behaviors |
| **Feels** | Emotional states with context | Frustrations, enthusiasms, anxieties |

To create an empathy map individually: use an A3 sheet or a digital document divided into 4 quadrants. Fill in each quadrant based on the research notes, using sticky notes or colored text. Contradictions between quadrants (e.g., says they like it, but feels frustration) often reveal the most important tensions to resolve.

**Tool: Persona**

A persona is a fictional character that represents a user archetype, built from multiple interviews and observations. While the empathy map captures a specific moment or situation, the persona describes recurring patterns of behavior, needs and goals of a group of users ([User Persona and Empathy Map: Key Differences | Userpilot](https://userpilot.com/blog/empathy-map-vs-persona/)).

A minimal persona should contain: fictional name, approximate age, life context relevant to the problem, main goals, main frustrations and a representative quote.

**Tool: User Journey Map**

While the empathy map focuses on one moment, the journey map describes the user's complete experience across multiple stages, identifying touchpoints, emotions at each step and opportunities for intervention. It is particularly useful for identifying **where** in the process the most critical problem occurs.

**Tool: POV (Point of View) Statement**

The POV statement turns observations into an actionable problem statement using the formula ([Define and Frame Your Design Challenge | IxDF](https://ixdf.org/literature/article/define-and-frame-your-design-challenge-by-creating-your-point-of-view-and-ask-how-might-we)):

```
[User] needs [need] because [insight].
```

Concrete example: "Older adults with arthritis need an easy way to open medication packaging because hand pain prevents them from taking their medication at the right time, creating a health risk."

The POV statement must always be user-oriented, not business-oriented. "We need to increase engagement by 20%" is not a valid POV — it is a business goal. A valid POV describes a real human need.

**Tool: "How Might We" Questions (HMW)**

From the POV, HMW questions are formulated that open the creative space for ideation without prescribing solutions. The structure is: "How might we [verb] [for whom] [goal]?"

Examples from the POV above:
- "How might we make medication packaging openable with one hand?"
- "How might we visually indicate the medication time without requiring reading?"
- "How might we turn opening the packaging into a gesture that does not require finger strength?"

The skill in formulating good HMW questions lies in calibrating the scope: questions that are too broad ("How might we improve the health of older adults?") do not give direction; questions that are too narrow ("How might we make a lighter cap?") limit creativity.

### Phase 3 — Ideate

**Objective:** Generate as many diverse ideas as possible in response to the HMW questions, deferring critical judgment.

This phase is where creativity expands. The central rule is quantity over quality at first — critical judgment is deliberately suspended so that unusual ideas can appear. The best ideas rarely emerge as first answers; they appear after the obvious answers have been exhausted.

**Technique: Crazy 8s**

Crazy 8s is a rapid sketching technique originally used in the Google Design Sprint that works equally well for individual use ([Crazy 8's - Design Sprint Kit | Google](https://designsprintkit.withgoogle.com/methodology/phase3-sketch/crazy-8s)):

1. Fold an A4 sheet in half three times to create 8 equal rectangles
2. Set a timer for 8 minutes
3. Sketch a different idea in each rectangle — one minute per idea
4. Sketch, do not write: shapes, arrows and quick notes work better than paragraphs

The goal is to force the brain beyond the first obvious answers. The ideas do not need to be good — they need to be different. At the end, you will have 8 possible directions, of which at least 2 or 3 usually deserve development.

**Technique: Worst Possible Idea**

This inversion technique asks you to deliberately generate the worst imaginable solutions to the problem. It serves two purposes: it unblocks the self-critical judgment that stalls ideation and it often reveals, by contrast, what makes a solution good ([The 5 Stages of Design Thinking | IxDF](https://ixdf.org/literature/article/5-stages-in-the-design-thinking-process)).

Individual procedure:
1. Spend 5 minutes listing the worst possible ideas for the HMW question
2. Pick the 3 worst and invert them: the opposite of each negative attribute is a potential positive attribute
3. Use the resulting positive attributes as input for serious ideation

**Technique: Brainwriting (individual)**

A silent variant of brainstorming that works especially well for individual use: write down an idea, then write a variation of it, then a variation of the variation. Repeat for 15 minutes. The chaining process generates ideas that free generation would not produce.

**Technique: Mash-up**

Randomly combine two unrelated concepts to generate unexpected solutions. Example: "medication packaging" + "reminder app" = packaging with a QR code that triggers a notification on the phone when opened. The randomness forces connections that linear thinking would block.

### Phase 4 — Prototype

**Objective:** Turn ideas into tangible, testable artifacts at the lowest possible cost and time.

The core philosophy of prototyping in Design Thinking is "build to think, not to present". A prototype is not a finished product — it is an object of investigation. The practical rule: do not invest more than one day of work in a prototype before testing it.

Prototyping forms for individual use:

- **Paper sketch:** For digital interfaces or simple physical products, hand drawings are enough for concept tests.
- **Storyboard:** A sequence of frames illustrating how the user would interact with the solution in a real scenario.
- **Wizard of Oz prototype:** Manually simulate the behavior of a technological solution during the test, without actually building the technology. Useful for validating the logic before any development.
- **Roleplay/enactment:** For services or processes, act out the experience with the other person taking the user's role.

### Phase 5 — Test

**Objective:** Observe how real people interact with the prototype, collect learnings and iterate.

Testing is not presenting — it is observing. The temptation is to explain the prototype to the user. The correct behavior is to hand over the prototype and observe where the person gets stuck, where they get confused, where they smile. The facilitator's silence is more valuable than explanations.

After each test session, record: what worked, what did not work, and one surprise (something unexpected). These three fields structure the iteration for the next cycle.

**The iterative cycle:** Test results often reveal new problems or reframe the original problem, sending the process back to the Define phase or even to Empathize. This is not a failure of the method — it is the method working correctly.

## Double Diamond — Design Council (2005)

The Double Diamond was developed by the British Design Council in 2003 and published in 2005. It is a visual model that represents the design process as two connected diamonds, each describing a cycle of divergent thinking (expansion) followed by convergent thinking (narrowing) ([The Double Diamond | Design Council](https://www.designcouncil.org.uk/our-resources/the-double-diamond/)).

### Visual and Conceptual Structure

```
Vague             Right              Solution
problem     →     problem      →     delivered
   [  Diamond 1  ]   [  Diamond 2  ]
    ↗ Discover ↘       ↗ Develop ↘
   diverge    converge  diverge    converge
               Define               Deliver
```

The first diamond answers the question: **"Are we solving the right problem?"**
The second diamond answers the question: **"Are we solving the problem the right way?"**

### The Four Phases

**Discover — Divergent Thinking**

Explore the problem broadly before assuming you know what it is. It involves user research, context analysis, benchmarking and field observation. The goal is to accumulate diverse perspectives and raw data without critical filtering yet.

Typical activities: interviews, ethnography, trend analysis, desk research, user diaries.

**Define — Convergent Thinking**

Synthesize the findings into a clear, focused design brief. This is the convergence point of the first diamond — from the volume of information collected, a precise definition of the real problem to be solved emerges.

Expected result: a design brief or challenge statement that will guide the entire second diamond.

**Develop — Divergent Thinking**

Generate and explore multiple potential solutions to the well-defined problem. This is the second moment of expansion — the more diverse the ideas generated, the greater the likelihood that an innovative solution will emerge.

Typical activities: ideation workshops, co-creation, exploratory prototyping, preliminary tests.

**Deliver — Convergent Thinking**

Select, refine and prepare the solution for real launch. Includes final tests, adjustments and implementation planning.

### Difference Compared to the 5-Phase Model

| Aspect | 5 Phases (d.school) | Double Diamond |
|--------|-------------------|----------------|
| Focus | Process and mindset | Visual structure and phases |
| Origin | Stanford / IDEO | British Design Council |
| Typical application | Education, product innovation | Organizational design, services |
| Granularity | High (specific tools) | Medium (broad phases) |
| Iteration | Explicit and central | Implicit |

The Double Diamond is especially useful as a **shared mental map** in projects with multiple stakeholders, since its visual simplicity makes it easier to communicate which phase the project is in.

## Design Sprint — Google Ventures (Jake Knapp)

The Design Sprint was created by Jake Knapp at Google in 2010 and refined at Google Ventures (GV) with portfolio startups such as Slack, Blue Bottle Coffee and 23andMe. It is documented in the book *Sprint: How to Solve Big Problems and Test New Ideas in Just Five Days* (2016) ([The Design Sprint | GV](https://www.gv.com/sprint/)).

### The Fundamental Principle

The Design Sprint compresses into 5 days what would normally take months: going from a critical strategic question to an answer based on real user evidence. The logic is: "learn without building and launching". Instead of spending months developing a product only to discover it does not work, the sprint produces a testable prototype in 4 days and tests it with real users on the 5th day.

### The Day-by-Day Process

**Monday — Map and Select the Target**

- Define the project's long-term goal
- Map the problem as a user flow (who → steps → goal)
- Interview internal experts ("Ask the Experts")
- Select a specific, manageable target from the map to solve this week

The day's result is a visual map of the challenge and a clear target.

**Tuesday — Generate Solutions**

- Review existing solutions for inspiration (Lightning Demos: 3 minutes per reference)
- Each participant sketches solutions individually in 4 steps: notes → ideas → crazy 8s → detailed solution sketch
- The process is individual and silent — no group influence on idea generation

**Wednesday — Decide and Create the Storyboard**

- Silent review of all sketches (Sticky Decision)
- Voting with sticker dots ("dot voting")
- Structured debate on the finalist solutions
- Creation of a 10 to 15 frame storyboard that will serve as the script for the prototype

**Thursday — Prototype**

- Build a realistic but superficial prototype in a single day
- The "fake it" philosophy: simulate only the user-facing surface, without building real infrastructure
- Tools: Keynote, Figma, paper, or any medium that allows simulating the experience

**Friday — Test with Real Users**

- Conduct 5 individual 60-minute interviews with previously recruited users
- Observe reactions without explaining the prototype
- Identify behavior patterns across the 5 users: what 3 or more people do is a significant data point

### Adaptation for Individual Use

The Design Sprint was designed for teams of 5 to 7 people with a "Decider" (decision maker). For individual use, the necessary adaptations are:

- **Monday:** The map and target selection are done by the individual, ideally with input from 1 or 2 people close to the problem.
- **Tuesday-Wednesday:** Solution generation and the decision are done individually. Use explicit criteria to select the idea to prototype, avoiding choosing the most comfortable one.
- **Thursday:** The prototype can be simpler — a paper storyboard or a simulation in Figma is enough.
- **Friday:** Conduct at least 3 test interviews, not 5. Three users are already enough to identify the most critical problems.

### Difference Compared to Classic Design Thinking

| Aspect | Design Thinking (d.school) | Design Sprint (GV) |
|--------|---------------------------|-------------------|
| Duration | Weeks to months | Fixed 5 days |
| Focus | Process and mindset | Result within a defined timeframe |
| Empathy phase | Deep, exploratory | Compressed (Monday) |
| Prototype | Iterative, multiple cycles | One prototype, tested once |
| Ideal for | Exploring complex problems | Quick validation of a specific hypothesis |

## Empathy Tools in Detail

### Empathy Interview — Practical Script

An effective empathy interview follows a progression of questions from general to specific ([Stanford d.school Design Thinking Bootleg](https://dschool.stanford.edu/tools/design-thinking-bootleg)):

1. **Opening:** "Tell me about yourself and [problem context]."
2. **Narrative:** "Describe the last time you [situation related to the problem]."
3. **Probing:** "Why did you do that?" / "How did you feel when...?"
4. **Values:** "What is most important to you in this situation?"
5. **Closing:** "If you could change one thing about this experience, what would it be?"

Rules during the interview: do not suggest answers, tolerate silence, ask "why?" at least three times in a chain to reach deep motivations.

### User Journey Map — Minimal Structure

A minimal journey map for individual use should have:

| Column | Content |
|--------|---------|
| Stages | The phases of the experience (e.g.: Discover, Try, Buy, Use, Renew) |
| Actions | What the user does at each stage |
| Thoughts | What goes through the user's mind |
| Emotions | Level of satisfaction (emotion curve, from frustrated to satisfied) |
| Opportunities | Where there is room to improve the experience |

## When to Use Each Framework

| Framework | Use when... | Avoid when... |
|-----------|--------------|----------------|
| Design Thinking (5 phases) | The problem is not well defined; there is time for user research; the solution needs deep human grounding | The deadline is very short; the problem is already well defined; there is no access to real users |
| Double Diamond | You need to communicate the process to stakeholders; you are structuring a larger design project; you want a shared mental map | You work alone without a need for external communication; you want specific tools (the Double Diamond is more structure than recipe) |
| Design Sprint | You need a quick answer to a specific hypothesis; you want to validate before investing in development; you have a week available and access to users | The problem is not yet defined enough to have a clear target; there are no real users available for testing on Friday |

## Common Criticisms and Responses

### Criticism 1: "Design Thinking does not produce disruptive innovation — it is repackaged common sense"

This criticism has partial basis. Design Thinking, as often practiced in corporate workshops, can result in incremental and superficial solutions when the empathy phase is compressed into a classroom exercise. The more substantial criticism, articulated in the Stanford Social Innovation Review, points out that the method rarely produces solutions to complex systemic problems because it is limited to the level of the symptoms perceived by users ([When Design Thinking Fails | SSIR Brasil](https://ssir.com.br/quando-o-design-thinking-falha/)).

**Practical response:** For individual use, the value of Design Thinking lies not in guaranteeing disruptive innovation, but in structuring the problem-solving process so that the generated solution actually answers a real need, not an assumption. Deep empathy, even if imperfect, produces better solutions than the absence of empathy.

### Criticism 2: "Empathy is impossible — you never truly understand the user"

The criticism is epistemologically legitimate: a designer rarely achieves complete empathy with a user from a radically different context. The field's response is that the goal is not perfect empathy, but systematic reduction of incorrect assumptions. Three well-conducted interviews always reveal something you did not know, even if they do not reveal everything ([Design Thinking: Definitions, Criticisms and Reflections | Medium](https://thaisleivas.medium.com/design-thinking-defini%C3%A7%C3%B5es-cr%C3%ADticas-e-reflex%C3%B5es-8fd2f574520f)).

### Criticism 3: "The process takes too much time"

For individual use, the frameworks can be scaled down dramatically:

- A compressed version of Design Thinking can be executed in 2 days: 3 hours of research + 1 hour of definition + 1 hour of ideation + 2 hours of prototype + 2 hours of testing.
- The Design Sprint was conceived precisely as a response to this criticism: 5 days with a tested result.
- The Double Diamond can be used merely as a conceptual lens, without changing the project's timeline.

### Criticism 4: "Design Thinking is conservative — it preserves the status quo"

This criticism, derived from the work of Natasha Iskander, points out that by centering on users' stated needs, Design Thinking tends to optimize what exists instead of imagining the radically different ([Design Thinking Is Fundamentally Conservative | IT Forum](https://itforum.com.br/o-design-thinking-e-fundamentalmente-conservador-diz-a-critica/)).

**Practical response:** The "Worst Possible Idea" technique and broadly formulated HMW questions are mechanisms internal to Design Thinking that try to work around this limitation. For more disruptive ideation, combine Design Thinking with techniques from other frameworks (TRIZ, SCAMPER) in the Ideate phase.

## Practical Example: Design Thinking in Individual Use

**Scenario:** You want to create a personal method for learning languages more consistently.

**Empathize:** Interview 3 friends who have tried and given up on learning a language. Ask about the exact moment they stopped. Use the empathy map to organize what each one said, thought, did and felt.

**Define:** POV: "Adults with a busy routine need a way to keep up daily language practice because inconsistency, not lack of interest, is the main reason for giving up." HMW: "How might we make language practice inevitable for those who already have an established routine?"

**Ideate:** Crazy 8s with the HMW question. Possible ideas: practice while showering (audio), switch the phone's language, practice with subtitles in movies you would already watch, gamification with bets among friends, tie the practice to an existing habit (breakfast).

**Prototype:** Choose the idea of tying practice to breakfast. Prototype: a sticky note on the coffee maker with 3 sentences in German to repeat while making coffee. Cost: zero. Creation time: 5 minutes.

**Test:** Practice for 5 days. Observe: how many times you actually did it, what made it difficult, what worked. Iterate the prototype based on what you learned.

## Key Takeaways

1. **Design Thinking is not a sequence — it is a set of mindsets.** Empathize, Define, Ideate, Prototype and Test are modes of operating that alternate according to what the problem demands, not steps to be completed in order.
2. **The most neglected phase is the most important.** The problem definition (POV + HMW) determines the quality of everything that follows. Investing time here prevents weeks of work in the wrong direction.
3. **For individual use, start with the viable minimum.** One empathy interview, one empathy map on paper, one Crazy 8s and one paper prototype are already enough for a first learning cycle.
4. **The Double Diamond is a map, not a recipe.** Use it to communicate which phase you are in and to make sure you have gone through the question "am I solving the right problem?" before generating solutions.
5. **The Design Sprint is the version with a deadline.** When you have a specific hypothesis and need an answer within a week, the sprint is more efficient than open-ended Design Thinking.
6. **Cheap, fast prototypes are superior to elaborate prototypes.** The more you invest in a prototype, the harder it is to discard it when the test shows that it does not work.
7. **The criticisms of Design Thinking are valid for irresponsible corporate use.** For reflective individual use, with genuine empathy and honest iteration, the method delivers what it promises: solutions better aligned with real needs.
